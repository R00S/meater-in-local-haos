# Baltic Herring × Smoker — Recipe Temperature Research

## Cut profile

Smoking Baltic herring (strömming) has its own Swedish name: the product is *böckling* (hot-smoked strömming or herring; the word is borrowed from Dutch, "little buck"). Larger strömming are the traditional choice for smoking — small summer fish are fried instead. Smoking here is primarily a flavour and preservation method: Swedish food-culture documentation records cold smoking at about 20 °C for 8–10 days (the oldest method) and hot smoking at 75–85 °C for a few hours, in both cases combined with salting or drying; the Estonian archive gives 70–80 °C for a few hours after a 10% brine and drying, and the Russian recipe distinguishes cold smoking (35–45 °C, 2 h) from hot smoking (90–110 °C, 30 min). Hot smoking coagulates the protein and gives the fish a cooked character. No fetched source states an internal pull temperature for the fish, and none is inferred here; the smoking-chamber figures are the sources' own stated process temperatures.

## Styckesprofil

Varmrökt strömming har ett eget svenskt namn: *böckling* (namnet är lånat från nederländska, "liten bock"). Större strömming är det traditionella valet för rökning — små sommarströmmingar steks i stället. Rökningen är framför allt smakförhöjande och konserverande: svensk matkulturdokumentation anger kallrökning vid ca 20 °C i 8–10 dagar (äldsta metoden) och varmrökning vid 75–85 °C under några timmar, i båda fallen kombinerat med saltning eller torkning; estniskt arkiv anger 70–80 °C under några timmar efter 10% saltlake och torkning, och ryskt recept skiljer kallrökning (35–45 °C, 2 h) från varmrökning (90–110 °C, 30 min). Varmrökning koagulerar proteinet och ger fisken kokt karaktär. Ingen hämtad källa anger innertemperatur för fisken; process-temperaturerna är källornas egna angivna rökningstemperaturer.

## Source recipes

### 1. Matkult.se (Institutet för språk och folkminnen material) — Konservering och tillagning: Rökning
**Inspired by**: https://www.matkult.se/fisk/konservering-och-tillagning.html (fetched 2026-10-23). Swedish food-culture documentation site ("Matkult"), with archival photographs credited to Isof (Institute for Language and Folklore); documents historical Swedish fish preservation and cooking methods rather than a single modern recipe.
**Serves**: — (method documentation) · **Prep**: salting/drying first (always combined with smoking) · **Cook**: hot smoke 75–85 °C for a few hours; or cold smoke ~20 °C for 8–10 days

**Ingredients** (as documented)
- Herring or strömming (*sill eller strömming*)
- Salt (smoking was "always combined with salting or drying")
- Wood smoke (traditional setups: Swedish fireplaces in the south; German-style fish smokehouses spread from the late 19th century — the era in which *böckling* arose)

**Method** (as documented)
1. Salt the fish (smoking alone does not preserve; the source states saltning, fermentering or torkning all preceded smoking).
2. For hot smoking (*varmrökning*): smoke at **75–85 °C for a few hours** — the protein coagulates and the fish takes on a cooked character. The source gives no fish internal temperature or explicit pull point; do not infer one from the process temperature.
3. For cold smoking (*kallrökning*): smoke at **about 20 °C for 8–10 days** — the oldest method; preserves with the aid of the preceding salting/drying.
4. The finished hot-smoked product is *böckling* (rökt sill eller strömming). The source states no final serving temperature.

### 3. Perekrestok — Копченая салака (Cold-smoked salaka)
**Inspired by**: https://www.perekrestok.ru/cat/na-zakusku/2163-kopchenaya-salaka-holodnogo-kopcheniya-na-mangale-ili-v-koptilne/ (fetched 2026-10-25). Russian food brand Пекарёва (Perekrestok) recipe. Species note: *салака* = *Clupea harengus membras* (Baltic herring) — verified via ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/Салака (fetched 2026-10-25).
**Serves**: not stated · **Prep**: brine 5–8 h (cold) or 25 min (hot) · **Cook**: cold smoke 2 h at 35–45 °C; hot smoke 30 min at 90–110 °C · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- Salt, sugar, bay leaf, peppercorns and/or other spices (amounts not stated)
- Alder chips (smoking wood)
- Salaka, amount not stated

**Method** (as documented)
1. Cold smoking (*холодное копчение*): brine the fish 5–8 hours in 10% brine, then dry 2–3 h in a draught. Smoke **2 hours at 35–45 °C**, alder chips; the page gives no fish internal temperature or explicit pull point, and none is inferred.
2. Hot smoking (*горячее копчение*): brine 25 min, smoke **30 min at 90–110 °C** (or in a cold smoker with a fire tray, ~20 min if cut into pieces). The process temperatures are the source's own chamber settings, not pull targets.
3. The source states no final serving temperature.

### 4. Pühajärve Kalapäev — Kuum suitsukala (Hot-smoked fish, festival recipe archive)
**Inspired by**: https://www.puhajarvekalapaev.ee/retseptid/retseptid/24-kuum-suitsukala/ (fetched 2026-10-25). Estonian fish-festival (Pühajärve Kalapäev) recipe archive, first published on Pühajärve Kalapäev's page; verbatim from the winning recipe of Urmas Õunapuu (Tabasalu Fishing Village). Species note: festival archive is not species-specific ("fish"); the same Estonian tradition's räim smoking is documented by paatmees.ee (kuum suitsutatud räim), recorded as a lead.
**Serves**: not stated · **Prep**: salt the fish in 10% brine · **Cook**: "a few hours" at 70–80 °C · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients** (as documented)
- Fresh fish
- 10% salt brine
- Alder, juniper and dried heather tips (smoking wood)

**Method** (as documented)
1. Salt the fish in 10% brine.
2. Dry at room temperature; develop "pellicle" (fish skin with a fine, sticky layer).
3. Smoke at **70–80 °C for a few hours** with alder, juniper and dried heather tips. The page gives no fish internal temperature or explicit pull point; do not infer one from the process temperature.
4. The source states no final serving temperature.

## What makes this method special

Hot smoking cooks and seasons Baltic herring at once: the 75–85 °C chamber is hot enough to coagulate the protein (giving böckling its cooked character) while the smoke deposits the aromatic layer that distinguishes it from plain salted fish. Because smoking was historically combined with salting, the texture is firmer than a bare fry; and because strömming is small and lean, a few hours in hot smoke is enough where salmonid fillets need longer. The method's Nordic specificity is captured by the product having its own name.

Smoked strömming is böckling: hot-smoked at 70–85 °C over a few hours after salting, it is a cooked, smoke-seasoned product with its own Swedish name — and the Estonian and Russian sources show the same brine-then-smoke pattern with overlapping chamber windows.
