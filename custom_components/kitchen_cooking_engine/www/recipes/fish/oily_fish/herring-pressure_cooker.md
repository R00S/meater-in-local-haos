# Herring × Pressure Cooker — Recipe Temperature Research

## Cut profile

This Japanese home-cooking recipe uses fresh herring, cut crosswise into sections, simmered under pressure in a seasoned liquid. The pressure-cooker method is chosen to soften the smaller bones and season the fish; the author says the method makes it easier to eat the fish bone-in, but explicitly notes that the thickest bones remain firm. The recipe runs for 40 minutes at pressure, then allows the cooker to cool and pressure to release before opening. It reports no internal temperature, measured endpoint, or rest temperature.

## Source recipes

### 1. がまおーじの母 / Cookpad JP — 圧力鍋でにしんの煮付け (Pressure-cooked simmered herring)
**Inspired by**: https://cookpad.com/jp/recipes/21694661 (fetched 2026-10-24). Japanese home-cook recipe for fresh herring, pressure-cooked with plum and soy-seasoned liquid.
**Serves**: not stated · **Prep**: clean and cut fish; time not stated · **Cook**: 40 minutes at pressure · **Rest**: leave sealed until pressure releases and pot cools

**Ingredients**
- 3 fresh herring
- 4 tbsp sake
- 4 tbsp mirin
- 3 tbsp light brown sugar
- 4 tbsp soy sauce
- 300 ml water
- 2 umeboshi

**Method**
1. Remove scales, head and tail; remove viscera (leave roe or milt if present), wash and cut each fish crosswise into three pieces.
2. Add sake, mirin, sugar, soy sauce, water and umeboshi to the pressure cooker; bring the liquid to a boil.
3. Add the herring pieces without stacking them. Close the lid and bring to pressure over high heat.
4. Once steam begins escaping, reduce to medium and pressure-cook for **40 minutes**.
5. Turn off the heat and leave the cooker undisturbed until it cools and pressure releases. The recipe states that this helps the flavour penetrate. It does not state an internal temperature or final serving temperature.

## What makes this method special

Pressure cooking exposes cut herring portions to a hot, moist environment above atmospheric boiling temperature, allowing liquid and seasoning to penetrate while softening some of the small bones. The recipe deliberately holds the fish in its closed cooker while pressure falls, continuing a gentle, contained cook and allowing the braising liquid to settle into the flesh. The author distinguishes small softened bones from the still-firm backbone, so the result should not be described as fully bone-softened. The source provides no measured fish temperature.
