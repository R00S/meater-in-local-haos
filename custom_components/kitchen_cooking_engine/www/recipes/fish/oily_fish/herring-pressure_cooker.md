# Herring × Pressure Cooker — Recipe Temperature Research

## Cut profile

Pressure cooking herring is done for one reason above all: to soften bone. A sealed cooker holds the contents above the atmospheric boiling point, so the small intramuscular bones and the backbone's collagen break down far faster than in an open pot, and a fish that is awkward to eat bone-in becomes a soft, spoonable one. The fetched sources show four national traditions using the method for four different ends: a Japanese seasoned pressure simmer (煮付け) that softens only the fine bones and leaves the thick ones firm; a Russian stovetop pressure-cooker canning (консервы в скороварке) that debones the fish first and processes it for shelf stability; a Korean pressure-rice-cooker "canned-style" batch (압력밥솥 통조림) that cooks in 15 minutes for the week's banchan; and a United States West Coast home-canning practice that pressure-cans Pacific herring in 12 oz jars. None of the four states an internal temperature, and none is inferred here.

## Source recipes

### 1. がまおーじの母 / Cookpad JP — 圧力鍋でにしんの煮付け (Pressure-cooked simmered herring)
**Inspired by**: https://cookpad.com/jp/recipes/21694661 (fetched 2026-10-24). Japanese home-cook recipe for fresh herring, pressure-cooked with plum and soy-seasoned liquid. Japanese tradition (Culinary Group A).
**Serves**: not stated · **Prep**: clean and cut fish; time not stated · **Cook**: 40 minutes at pressure · **Rest**: leave sealed until pressure releases and pot cools

**Ingredients**
- 3 fresh herring
- 4 tbsp sake
- 4 tbsp mirin
- 3 tbsp light brown sugar
- 4 tbsp soy sauce
- 300 ml water
- 2 umeboshi

**Method**
1. Remove scales, head and tail; remove viscera (leave roe or milt if present), wash and cut each fish crosswise into three pieces.
2. Add sake, mirin, sugar, soy sauce, water and umeboshi to the pressure cooker; bring the liquid to a boil.
3. Add the herring pieces without stacking them. Close the lid and bring to pressure over high heat.
4. Once steam begins escaping, reduce to medium and pressure-cook for **40 minutes**.
5. Turn off the heat and leave the cooker undisturbed until it cools and pressure releases. The recipe states that this helps the flavour penetrate.

**Probe and endpoint**: no probe step; **no internal temperature stated**. The author reports that the method makes the fish easier to eat bone-in but that the thickest bones remain firm — the bone state, not a temperature, is the stated endpoint.

### 2. Вера Кириленко / Food.ru — Консервированная сельдь в скороварке (Canned herring in a pressure cooker)
**Inspired by**: https://food.ru/recipes/52801-seld-konservirovannaja-na-skorovarke (fetched 2026-10-25). Russian recipe site Food.ru; reader-and-contributor recipe by Вера Кириленко, filed as Russian cuisine (Кухня: русская), published 2021-08-07. Hot-method home canning of herring in a stovetop pressure cooker. Russian tradition (Culinary Group D).
**Serves**: 5 herring, about 1.75 kg · **Prep**: sterilise jars, brew and cool the tea, gut and portion the fish; listed kitchen time 2 h, total "ready in" 5 h · **Cook**: 1 h at pressure · **Rest**: 1 h standing in the closed cooker, then cool the sealed jars in a dark warm place

**Ingredients**
- 5 herring, about 1750 g
- 40 g salt
- 6 black peppercorns, about 6 g
- 4 bay leaves, about 4 g
- 600 g water (3 cups)
- 190 g refined sunflower oil (1 cup)
- 6 g black tea (2 tsp), brewed strong

**Method**
1. Sterilise the jars in advance. Wash the herring, gut it, remove the large bones, head, fins, tail and scales, rinse again, dry on paper and cut into small pieces.
2. Brew the tea bags in boiling water and leave them in until the water has dropped to **40 °C**.
3. Lay the herring in the pressure cooker, salt it, add the pepper and bay, then pour over the warm strong tea and finally the oil. Do not stir.
4. Put the cooker on maximum heat and wait for it to signal that pressure is reached. Then reduce to the minimum and cook gently for **1 hour**.
5. Switch off the heat and leave the contents for a further **1 hour**. Then seal the herring into the jars and leave them to cool in a dark, warm place.
6. Serve with vegetables, mushrooms, potatoes or buckwheat.

**Probe and endpoint**: no probe step; **no internal temperature stated**. The 40 °C figure in step 2 is the temperature of the tea poured over the raw fish, not a doneness target. The fish arrives already deboned of its large bones, so bone softening is not the stated endpoint — shelf-stable canning is.

### 3. 진담그미 / Naver Blog — 청어(꽁치) 통조림만들기 (Making herring/saury "canned" fish)
**Inspired by**: https://blog.naver.com/jinyanggo/203642714 (fetched 2026-10-25; mobile view https://m.blog.naver.com/jinyanggo/203642714). Korean home-cooking blog by 진담그미, published 2014-02-03; the method was passed on from a Gangwon-do cook via the author's sister. Korean tradition (Culinary Group A).
**Serves**: 10 small fish, portioned into about 5 freezer bags · **Prep**: gut, remove head and tail fin; time not stated · **Cook**: 15 minutes at pressure (10 minutes if the fish is fresh rather than partly dried) · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 10 herring (*cheong-eo*), gutted, heads and tail fins removed — the contributor was not certain of her own fish and thought it was either herring or capelin
- 2 tbsp soy sauce (집간장, home-brewed soy)
- 2 tbsp gochugaru (Korean chilli powder)
- 1 tbsp minced garlic
- Black pepper, a little
- 1 tbsp plum extract (매실엑기스)
- About 1/2 cup water

**Method**
1. Trim the fish: remove head, tail fin and innards.
2. Mix the soy sauce, gochugaru, garlic, pepper and plum extract, add about half a cup of water, and pour over the fish in the pressure rice cooker (*apryeok bapsot*). Do not stir.
3. Close the lid and cook under pressure for **15 minutes**; the author says 10 minutes is enough for fresh fish and that she added 5 minutes because hers was partly dried.
4. The result is described as canned-fish texture: the fine bones become soft, so the batch can be frozen in single-portion bags and dropped into kimchi stew through the year. The author notes that overcooking makes the fish fall apart when the stew is boiled.
5. She adds that the same method works for herring, saury (*kkongchi*), capelin and mackerel.

**Probe and endpoint**: no probe step; **no internal temperature stated**. The endpoint the source gives is bone texture — the small bones soften to canned-fish softness — and a time, not a temperature.

### 4. Eddie (NorCal Kayak Anglers) — Canning Herring 2023
**Inspired by**: https://www.norcalkayakanglers.com/index.php?topic=99861.0 (fetched 2026-10-25; print view https://www.norcalkayakanglers.com/index.php?action=printpage;topic=99861.0). First-person pressure-canning recipe and tasting report on a California kayak-fishing community forum, opened 2023-01-19 and followed up 2023-02-05 and 2023-11-16. The fish is Pacific herring (*Clupea pallasii*). United States tradition (Culinary Group B).
**Serves**: 7 × 12 oz ("12 oz textured jelly") jars · **Prep**: scale, gut or fillet, scrub the bloodline, then brine 1 h · **Cook**: 100 minutes at 10 lb pressure · **Rest**: cool and store in a cool dark place; the first jar was opened about 10 days later

**Ingredients**
- Pacific herring, scaled and gutted, or filleted — the contributor tried both and preferred fillets
- Brine: 1 tbsp salt per quart (about 1 litre) of water, 1 hour
- Per jar: 1 bay leaf, 1/8 tsp salt, 2–3 peppercorns, 1–2 tbsp olive oil
- Flavour variants, per the contributor's notes: 1 tbsp Louisiana hot sauce + 1/8 tsp paprika; 1 tsp French's mustard + 1/4 tsp dill; blended fresh tomato + 2 tbsp oil + 1 tsp paprika + 1/8 tsp sugar; 1 tbsp soy sauce + 1 tbsp lemon + 1/8 tsp sugar

**Method**
1. Scale the fish, remove head and innards, or fillet it; scrub out the bloodline. The contributor found filleting faster than gutting.
2. Brine for **1 hour** in 1 tbsp salt per quart of water, then drain.
3. Season each 12 oz jar before adding fish: bay leaf, 1/8 tsp salt, 2–3 peppercorns, 1–2 tbsp olive oil, plus one of the flavour variants.
4. Pack the herring in, leaving 1 inch (about 2.5 cm) of headspace below the lid.
5. Process at **10 lb pressure for 100 minutes**.
6. Cool, then store in a cool dark place. Tasted on 2023-02-05: the mustard-dill and tomato-paprika jars were judged a success in flavour and consistency, and "the bones [were] completely chewable". A jar opened on 2023-11-16 was still good.

**Probe and endpoint**: no probe step; **no internal temperature stated**. Two other contributors in the same thread report their canned herring turning mushy, and one links the National Center for Home Food Preservation's 110-minute figure for smoked fish — the thread is a record of practice and outcome, not an authoritative canning standard.

## Temperature consensus

| Tradition | Preferred doneness name | Final serving temp |
|-----------|------------------------|--------------------|
| Japanese (Cookpad JP — にしんの煮付け) | Not stated — 40 min at pressure, held until pressure releases; fine bones softened, thick bones still firm | Not stated |
| Russian (Food.ru — canned herring in a pressure cooker) | Not stated — 1 h at pressure + 1 h standing; large bones removed before cooking | Not stated |
| Korean (Naver blog — 압력밥솥 canned-style herring) | Not stated — 15 min at pressure from partly dried fish, 10 min from fresh; bones soft as canned fish | Not stated |
| United States (NorCal Kayak Anglers — pressure-canned Pacific herring) | Not stated — 10 lb / 100 min; bones "completely chewable" | Not stated |

**Observed range: none.** Not one of the four sources states an internal or final serving temperature, so there is no range to report and no figure is offered in its place; with no published pull point in any tradition, none can be mapped onto the `medium` or `well_done` bands, and `recommended_doneness` is left unchanged rather than guessed from time, pressure and bone texture. What the four agree on is the shape of the endpoint: a period under pressure, followed by an unprobed judgement that the bones have softened. They diverge sharply on that period — 15 minutes in the Korean pressure rice cooker, 40 minutes in the Japanese simmer, 100 minutes in the American canner — and the divergence tracks purpose rather than philosophy: a quick banchan batch, a seasoned simmer, and a shelf-stable jar each buy a different amount of bone breakdown. The starting state differs too, which is why the Korean time is the shortest: partly dried fish in a small pressure rice cooker, against raw fish in a large canner. Coverage is Groups A (Japan and Korea), B (United States) and D (Russia); no Group C source was found for this method, and that gap is stated rather than filled by stretching a different species or method into the leaf.

## What makes this method special

A pressure cooker holds its contents at roughly 115–121 °C instead of 100 °C, and that extra 15–20 °C is what attacks herring's bones: collagen in the fine intramuscular bones and the backbone hydrolyses into gelatin, so the fish goes from fiddly to fork-tender without the flesh being cooked to mush by long boiling at ambient pressure. Herring's high fat content helps here — the rendered oil keeps the flesh lubricated while the connective tissue breaks down, and both the Russian and American sources add extra oil (a full cup in the Russian pot, a tablespoon or two per jar in the American) which also limits oxidation of that fat during storage. The four sources map the technique's range: 15 minutes softens the fine bones in a Korean pressure rice cooker, 40 minutes in a Japanese simmer softens them but leaves the thickest bones firm, and a full hour to 100 minutes produces a jar-stable product whose bones can be chewed outright. The Russian recipe sidesteps the question entirely by removing the large bones before cooking. What no tradition does is measure the fish.

## Summary

Pressure cooking is the method that makes herring's bones edible: the sealed pot pushes the cooking temperature well past boiling, the collagen in the small bones turns to gelatin, and a fish normally eaten around the skeleton can be eaten through it. That makes it an excellent match for this cut, and a better one than long simmering, which dries the flesh before the bones give way. The cost is control — the flesh is soft, and the sources disagree by a factor of six on how long it should stay under pressure, from a fifteen-minute Korean batch to a hundred-minute canned jar, so the cook is choosing a texture rather than following a rule. Judge it by the bones, and pull early rather than late.
