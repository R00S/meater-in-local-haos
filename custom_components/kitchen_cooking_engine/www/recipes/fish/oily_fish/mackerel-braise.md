# Mackerel × Braise — Recipe Temperature Research

## Cut profile

A braise cooks mackerel in a small amount of liquid under a lid, so the fish is finished by
conducted heat from a sauce it sits in rather than by radiant heat or a water bath. That matters
for this cut in three ways: the fat renders into the sauce and flavours it, the acid in Korean
gochujang/soy sauces and in Vietnamese caramel and Italian tomato cuts the richness, and the fish
is thin enough that it is done long before the accompanying vegetables or the sauce reduction —
which is why every source in this collection either layers the fish above the vegetables or adds
it late, and why the liquid is measured to come part-way up the fish rather than cover it.
Sources come from three traditions here (Korean ×2, Italian, Vietnamese); the Italian source is a
shallow covered sauce-cook rather than a true submerge-braise, and is recorded as such.

## Source recipes

### 1. Imsun and Emily Miller / Fishful Future — Korean Spicy Braised Mackerel
**Inspired by**: https://www.fishfulfuture.com/food-from-the-sea-comida-del-mar/imsun-eng (fetched 2026-10-24). Korean tradition; authors identify the fish as Atlantic mackerel and cut the whole fish into 3-inch steaks, then halve along the backbone.

**Serves**: 4 · **Prep**: not stated · **Cook**: 15 min at low simmer · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 2 large whole Atlantic mackerel; 1 large daikon radish; 1 onion; 1 bundle green onions; optional bundle red Korean chiles; 1 cup water
- Seasoning: 1/2 cup soy sauce; 2 tbsp sesame oil; 1 tbsp gochujang; 2 tbsp white sugar; 4 tbsp gochugaru; 5 large garlic cloves; 1 thumb ginger

**Method**
1. Mince garlic and ginger and mix with the seasoning ingredients. Slice onion and green onions; slice peeled daikon.
2. Clean the fish, remove fins and optionally heads, gut, cut into 3-inch steaks and halve along the backbone.
3. Layer onion and daikon in a deep pot, add the fish and cover with the seasoning. Add green onion and the optional chiles.
4. Add 1 cup water, bring to a boil, then reduce to a low simmer for 15 minutes or until fully cooked.
5. Serve hot with rice.

**Probe and endpoint**: no probe step; **no internal temperature stated**. Endpoint is 15 minutes at low simmer or fully cooked.

### 2. Hyegyoung K. Ford / Beyond Kimchee — Korean Braised Mackerel and Radish
**Inspired by**: https://www.beyondkimchee.com/braised-korean-mackerel/ (fetched 2026-10-24). Korean tradition; ingredient line: “1 whole mackerel fish, well-cleaned and cut into 3–4 pieces.” Species not stated.

**Serves**: 3 · **Prep**: 15 min (+10–15 min rice-water soak) · **Cook**: 20 min · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 1 whole mackerel, cleaned and cut into 3–4 pieces; 3 cups rice water; 10 oz Korean radish; 1/2 large onion; 1 optional fresh chile; 1 green onion
- Sauce: 2 tbsp gochugaru; 3 tbsp soy sauce; 1 tbsp Korean tuna sauce; 2 garlic cloves; 2 tsp ginger paste; 1/2 tsp sugar; 1/2 tsp pepper; 1/2 cup water

**Method**
1. Soak the fish pieces in rice-starch water 10–15 minutes; drain.
2. Mix gochugaru, soy sauce, tuna sauce, garlic, ginger, sugar and pepper.
3. Layer onion and radish in a shallow braising pot; spread half the sauce over the radish. Add the fish and the remaining sauce.
4. Add 1/2 cup water, cover, bring to a boil, then reduce the heat to low and simmer 15–20 minutes until the radish softens.
5. Garnish with chopped green onion and serve warm with rice.

**Probe and endpoint**: no probe step; **no internal temperature stated**. Endpoint is soft radish and 15–20 minutes.

### 3. Gennaro Contaldo / BBC Food — Sgombro alla Pizzaiola
**Inspired by**: https://www.bbc.co.uk/food/recipes/mackerel_pizzaiola_sauce_40911 (fetched 2026-10-25). Italian tradition; *sgombro* is the Italian market name for Atlantic mackerel. From *Saturday Kitchen*; the pizzaiola family of sauces is the Italian shallow covered tomato-cook normally applied to meat.

**Serves**: 4 · **Prep**: under 30 min · **Cook**: 10–30 min · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 2 tbsp extra virgin olive oil; 2 garlic cloves, finely chopped; 1/4 red chilli, finely chopped (optional)
- 400 g / 14 oz baby plum tomatoes, halved; 20 g / 3/4 oz capers; 10 pitted black olives; pinch dried oregano
- A handful fresh flatleaf parsley, finely chopped
- 4 mackerel fillets (about 500 g / 1 lb 2 oz in total), skinned or skin-on; sea salt

**Method**
1. Heat the oil in a large, deep frying pan; lightly fry the garlic and chilli over medium heat for a minute.
2. Stir in the tomatoes, capers, olives, oregano, half the parsley and a little salt; stir-fry over medium-high heat a couple of minutes. Reduce the heat, cover, and cook **5 minutes**.
3. Uncover, lay the mackerel fillets **on top of** the sauce, drizzle over 2 tbsp warm water, cover again and cook a further **5 minutes**.
4. Uncover, raise the heat and cook about **2 minutes** to evaporate most of the liquid. Off the heat, scatter the remaining parsley.
5. Serve with sautéed rosemary potatoes (potatoes boiled until tender, then crisped in olive oil with garlic and rosemary).

**Probe and endpoint**: no probe step; **no internal temperature stated**. Doneness is by time and by the sauce reducing; the fillets sit on the sauce, not submerged in it.

---

### 4. Nguyen Thanh Mai / Cookpad — Cá Thu Kho Tộ (Braised Mackerel in Clay Pot)
**Inspired by**: https://cookpad.com/eng/recipes/13734814 (fetched 2026-10-25). Vietnamese tradition, Nha Trang home cook; originally published on Cookpad Vietnam. *Cá thu* is king/spanish mackerel; 2 fish slices (thickness not stated).

**Serves**: not stated · **Prep**: not stated · **Cook**: not stated ("until tender and the sauce thickens") · **Rest**: none

**Ingredients**
- 2 slices mackerel
- Shallots and garlic (quantities not stated)
- 1 tbsp pork fat
- 2 tbsp sugar; 1 tbsp oyster sauce; 1 tbsp seasoning powder; 1 tbsp fish sauce; sliced chilli; 1 tsp black pepper; 3 tbsp water
- Boiling water, enough to cover **half** the thickness of the fish
- Chopped green onions

**Method**
1. Rinse the mackerel slices and pat completely dry. Sauté chopped shallots and garlic in 1 tbsp pork fat until fragrant.
2. Off the heat, transfer them to a clay pot and add the sugar, oyster sauce, seasoning powder, fish sauce, chilli, pepper and 3 tbsp water. Return to the heat and stir to combine.
3. Add the mackerel slices, flip once quickly to coat both sides, then leave undisturbed. Pour in enough boiling water to cover half the fish's thickness and simmer over **low heat**.
4. Simmer over low heat until the fish is tender and the sauce thickens. Add chopped green onions and transfer to a plate.

**Probe and endpoint**: no probe step; **no internal temperature stated**, and no clock time — the endpoint is the thickened, reduced caramel and tender fish.

## Method note

Across the three traditions the liquid quantity is the method variable, not the temperature: the
Korean sources build a vegetable bed so the fish never touches the pot floor and add 1/2–1 cup of
water; the Italian source uses the tomatoes' own juice plus 2 tbsp of water and cooks the fillets
on top of the sauce; the Vietnamese source pours boiling water to exactly half the fish's thickness
and reduces it to a caramel. None sears the fish first, and none states an internal temperature or
a numeric doneness point. Two of four sources give a clock (15 min; 20 min at low simmer) and both
are bounded by the *vegetable* — soft radish — rather than by the fish, which is the practical
warning this method carries for a thin cut: the mackerel is done before the braise is.

No consensus range can be stated: the observed set of published final temperatures is empty. The
tradition split is therefore about sauce architecture (soy-gochugaru, tomato-caper, fish-sauce
caramel), not doneness.

## Summary

Braising turns mackerel into something almost unlike grilled mackerel: the flesh loosens into soft
flakes and the fat that would crisp on a grill instead dissolves into the sauce, so the dish tastes
rich rather than sharp. It is a strong match for this cut's oiliness and a poor match for its
delicacy — all four sources handle the fish by leaving it undisturbed, layering it above a
vegetable bed or resting it on a sauce, because stirring would break it apart. The cook trades the
crisp skin of dry heat for a sauce that carries the fish's fat, and gains a dish that keeps and
reheats. The key takeaway: keep the liquid below the top of the fish, cook it gently, and judge
doneness by the vegetables, not by the clock.
