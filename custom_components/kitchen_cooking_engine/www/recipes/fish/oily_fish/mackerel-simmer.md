## Cut profile

Simmering is the great mackerel method of East and South Asia: the fish is gently poached in a
flavoured broth — miso-sake in Japan, spiced coconut-tamarind water in Kerala — and the oily
flesh, which would dry under direct heat, stays silky while absorbing the liquid. Both fetched
sources drop the fish into an already-boiling seasoned liquid and then hold it below a full
boil (Japanese drop-lid simmering; covered Indian curry pot), and neither sears the fish
first — which is why this is filed as `simmer` rather than `braise`. Both cook the fish
completely; neither states an internal temperature.

## Source recipes

### 1. Saba Misoni (Mackerel Simmered in Miso) — Just One Cookbook
**Inspired by**: https://www.justonecookbook.com/saba-misoni-simmered-mackerel-in-miso-sauce/ (fetched 2026-10-24). Namiko Hirasawa Chen, Just One Cookbook; Japanese tradition.

**Serves**: 4 · **Prep**: 10 min · **Cook**: 20 min · **Rest**: 30 min cooling (page lists total 30 min, excluding the rest)

**Ingredients**
- 2 mackerel (saba) fillets (13 oz / 374 g; from 1 mackerel)
- 4 Tbsp water
- 4 Tbsp sake
- 4 Tbsp miso, divided (2 Tbsp for the simmering sauce; 2 Tbsp added later)
- 2 Tbsp mirin
- 2 Tbsp sugar
- 1 knob fresh ginger, divided: half sliced for cooking, half julienned for garnish
- 1 tsp soy sauce
- Optional: kinome leaf, quantity not stated

**Method**
1. Peel the ginger. Slice half thinly for cooking and cut the rest into julienne for garnish.
2. Cut each fillet in half diagonally and make a shallow cross-cut in the skin on the thickest part.
3. Bring a medium pot of water to a boil. Briefly **blanch** the fillets one at a time; transfer to an ice bath, clean away blood or impurities, and set aside.
4. In a saucepan, combine the 4 Tbsp water, 4 Tbsp sake, 2 Tbsp mirin, 2 Tbsp sugar, 2 Tbsp miso and the sliced ginger. Bring to a boil over medium heat, stirring.
5. Lay the fish in a single layer, skin-side up. Reduce to **medium-low**, cover with an *otoshibuta* (drop lid), and gently **simmer 13–15 minutes**.
6. Remove the fish briefly. Add the remaining 2 Tbsp miso and dissolve it in the sauce.
7. Return the fish to the sauce and drizzle with the 1 tsp soy sauce. Tilt the pot to distribute the sauce and spoon it over the fish.
8. Turn off the heat, cover with a lid, and cool **30 minutes**.
9. Reheat uncovered over medium heat, spooning sauce over the fish. Serve with the julienned ginger and optional kinome.

**Probe and endpoint**: no probe step. **no internal temperature stated** — the endpoint is time (13–15 min simmer) plus the visual of the sauce glaze.

### 2. Mackerel Fish Curry (Ayala Curry) — Shalini / Something's Cooking
**Inspired by**: https://somethingiscooking.com/2015/06/01/mackerel-fish-curry-ayala-curry/ (fetched 2026-10-24). Shalini, Something's Cooking; Indian (Kerala-style) tradition, red curry with coccum (kudampuli), served with tapioca or rice.

**Serves**: not stated · **Prep**: not stated · **Cook**: 10–15 min after adding the fish · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 500 g medium mackerel (ayala), cleaned and cut into medium-sized pieces
- 4 pieces coccum, cleaned and soaked
- 1/4 cup water for soaking coccum (the method says 1/2 cup — discrepancy as published)
- 2 Tbsp coconut oil, for the pan
- 1 Tbsp finely chopped ginger
- 4 cloves finely chopped garlic
- 4–5 finely chopped shallots
- 2 green chillies, slit
- 2 sprigs curry leaves (plus "few" for sautéing and fresh leaves for garnish, unquantified)
- 3 Tbsp chilli powder
- 1 Tbsp coriander powder
- 1/2 tsp turmeric powder
- 1/4 tsp fenugreek powder
- 1/2 tsp black pepper powder
- Salt, to taste
- Water, amount not stated
- 1 tsp coconut oil, added at the end

**Method**
1. Mix the spice powders with 1–2 Tbsp water to make a paste. Soak the coccum in water.
2. Heat the oil in a pan, preferably a clay pot. Add the ginger, garlic, shallots, slit green chillies and some curry leaves; sauté.
3. When the aromatics turn golden brown, add the spice paste and roast until its raw smell goes away.
4. Add water and salt to taste, then the soaked coccum and its water. Mix and bring to a **boil**.
5. Add the fish, **cover, and cook 10–15 minutes**.
6. Adjust the salt. When the gravy thickens, turn off the heat, add 1 tsp coconut oil and garnish with fresh curry leaves. (The author advises rotating the pan rather than stirring, to avoid breaking the fish.)

**Probe and endpoint**: no probe step. **no internal temperature stated** — the endpoint is time (10–15 min covered) and gravy thickness.

Neither fetched simmer source states an internal temperature; the observed numeric range does
not exist and none is inferred. Both sources converge on **complete doneness** by time and by
the sauce's own reduction — in simmering, the fish cannot exceed the liquid's temperature, so
the endpoint is bounded by the broth's simmer (roughly 85–95 °C) regardless. The variation
between the two sources is in technique rather than doneness: the Japanese source controls
evaporation with a drop lid and finishes with a two-stage miso addition; the Indian source
controls it with a covered pot and a souring agent (coccum) that firms the flesh.

## What makes this method special

Simmering plays to mackerel's weakness — its fragility — by removing direct heat entirely: the
fish cooks buoyed in liquid, so the oily flesh never sticks, breaks or dries, and the fat it
leaches enriches the broth instead of burning. The Japanese blanch-and-rinse step and the
Indian spice-paste roasting both build a clean braising liquid before the fish enters it.
For a cut that overcooks in seconds in a pan, the simmer's gentle, bounded heat is the most
forgiving method in this collection.

## Summary

Simmering turns mackerel's fragility into an advantage: the fish poaches gently in miso broth or spiced curry, staying silky where dry heat would break it. Japanese and Indian kitchens converge on the same architecture — seasoned liquid first, fish in last, time-based finish. It is arguably the best structural match for the cut, though both sources leave the endpoint unmeasured.
