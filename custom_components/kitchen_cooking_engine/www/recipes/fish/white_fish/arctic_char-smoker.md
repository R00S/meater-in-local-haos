# Arctic Char × Smoker — Recipe Temperature Research

> **Collection status: incomplete.** This leaf holds **two verified arctic-char smoking records** and two
> general fish-smoking authorities, against a project minimum of four char-specific sources. No
> temperature consensus is asserted below, because the analysis gate forbids drawing one from an
> incomplete collection. What follows is the collected evidence, the searches that found nothing, and the
> two candidates that were rejected — so the next pass starts from here rather than from scratch.

## Cut profile

Smoking is the traditional Scandinavian fate of a char, and the reason is anatomical: char carries 5–10 g
of fat per 100 g, enough for the flesh to survive a long, dry, salty process without becoming leather, but
not so much that it behaves like salmon. The small size is the other half of the story — a 400–800 g whole
fish finishes in the smoke in the same time a salmon fillet takes to *start* — which is why the Swedish
guidance here talks about ten minutes in a box smoker and the Canadian recipe about five hours in a
cabinet.

That difference is not a disagreement about char; it is the difference between two things both called
"smoking". A box smoker (*röklåda*, *abu-rök*) burns a spirit burner under a tray of chips and cooks the
fish in about ten minutes at 60–80 °C; an electric cabinet holds 65–77 °C for hours and dries and cures the
flesh on the way. The two produce different products from the same fish, and they do not share a target
temperature — which is exactly why this leaf cannot yet state a band.

## Styckesprofil

Rökning är rödingens traditionella öde i Skandinavien, och skälet är anatomiskt: röding har 5–10 g fett per
100 g, tillräckligt för att köttet ska klara en lång, torr och salt process utan att bli läder, men inte så
mycket att den beter sig som lax. Den lilla storleken är andra halvan — en hel fisk på 400–800 g blir klar i
röken på samma tid som en laxfilé behöver för att *börja* — vilket är varför den svenska vägledningen talar
om tio minuter i röklåda och det kanadensiska receptet om fem timmar i ett rökskåp.

Det är inte oenighet om röding; det är skillnaden mellan två saker som båda kallas rökning. En röklåda
bränner en spritbrännare under spån och tillagar fisken på cirka tio minuter vid 60–80 °C; ett elektriskt
rökskåp håller 65–77 °C i timmar och torkar och konserverar köttet på vägen.

## Source recipes

### 1. Maui Hickory Smoked Arctic Char — Bradley Smoker (Canada)
**Inspired by**: https://www.bradleysmoker.com/blogs/recipes-fish-seafood/maui-hickory-smoked-arctic-char (fetched 2026-10-26). Recipe blog of Bradley Smoker (British Columbia); written in the first person by the person who cooked it, on a specific smoker. **Culinary Group B**.
**Serves**: not stated · **Prep**: 10–12 h brine + 2–3 h drying · **Cook**: 5–6 h · **Rest**: ~1 h to room temperature

**Ingredients**
- **Brine**: 4 cups water, ⅓ cup kosher salt, 1 cup brown sugar, ¾ cup peach schnapps
- Arctic char, cut into 2-inch-wide pieces
- Maui ribs marinade sauce, ground black pepper
- Hickory bisquettes

**Method**
1. Cut the char into 2-inch-wide pieces and brine them **skin side up for 10–12 hours**. *(verbatim)*
2. Wash the fish, pat it dry, and put it on a wire rack in a cool place for **2–3 hours**. *(verbatim)*
3. Brush the fish with the Maui marinade and add fresh ground black pepper. *(verbatim)*
4. Smoke at **150 °F for 2 hours**, brushing again with marinade after the first hour. *(verbatim)*
5. Raise to **160 °F for 2 hours**. *(verbatim)*
6. Finish at **170 °F for 1 to 2 hours, until the internal temperature reaches 130/140 °F**. *(verbatim)*
7. Remove from the smoker and let it cool to room temperature (about 1 hour) before vacuum-sealing. *(verbatim)*

**Recorded temperature**: **internal 130–140 °F (54–60 °C)**, with ambients of 66 → 71 → 77 °C. The author's purpose is stated: to vacuum-seal and keep, i.e. a preserving product, not a hot dinner plate.

### 2. Varmröka fisk, enkelt och gott — Bengt Keatler Olsson, Svenskt Fiske (Sweden)
**Inspired by**: https://svensktfiske.se/varmroka-fisk-enkelt-och-gott/ (fetched 2026-10-26). Svenskt Fiske, article by Bengt Keatler Olsson ("Text och foto"), published 8 August 2022. **Culinary Group D**.
**Serves**: not stated · **Prep**: 1–2 days brining · **Cook**: ~10 min in a box smoker · **Rest**: none stated

**Method** (a technique article, not a single recipe)
1. Almost all fatty fish smoke well because fat carries flavour — "exempelvis lax, makrill, **röding**, öring, regnbåge och sill är säkra kort". *(verbatim)*
2. Salt either dry or in brine; a suitable brine strength is **7–11 %**, and a salt meter helps. *(verbatim)*
3. A day in brine can be enough; small whole fish can instead be rubbed with salt and left about two days in the fridge. Dry the fish before smoking. *(paraphrase)*
4. Suitable woods are alder, oak, apple, cherry and maple — **not** birch, pine or spruce; fresh juniper is a classic. *(verbatim)*
5. **A suitable temperature for hot smoking is around 60–80 °C.** In a box smoker there is little to adjust: light the burner and crack the lid or vents to let steam out. *(verbatim)*
6. **The fish is hot-smoked in about ten minutes**, depending somewhat on its size. *(verbatim)*
7. In more advanced smokers it can be smoked more slowly at a slightly lower temperature so it keeps more of its juiciness. *(paraphrase)*

**Recorded temperature**: **ambient 60–80 °C, about ten minutes** — and **no internal temperature at all**. This is the Swedish box-smoker practice in its own words, and it judges doneness by time and appearance, not by a probe.

### 3. Smoking Fish at Home (FNH-00325) — University of Alaska Fairbanks, Cooperative Extension Service (United States)
**Inspired by**: https://www.uaf.edu/ces/publications/database/food/smoking-fish.php (fetched 2026-10-26). UAF Cooperative Extension Service publication FNH-00325, a food-safety bulletin for home smokers. **Culinary Group B**.
**Applies to**: any fish species — this is the safety authority, not a char recipe

**Method / rules as published**
1. For hot-smoked products, **the internal temperature of the fish is raised to at least 160 °F (71 °C)** for bacterial reduction; this also exceeds the 150 °F required to kill parasites. *(verbatim)*
2. Only fish intended for a **cold-smoke** process must be frozen beforehand, because hot smoking already exceeds the parasite-killing temperature. *(paraphrase)*
3. A recommended brine is **1½ pounds of salt per gallon of water**, chilled to 38 °F or lower; brine 20 minutes to 3 hours depending on taste. *(verbatim)*
4. Do not reuse brine; make fresh brine for each batch. *(verbatim)*
5. Equilibrate and dry the fish below 38 °F; a 1-inch-thick fillet needs 6–8 hours, and the surface must become tacky — the **pellicle** — before it goes in the smoker. *(verbatim)*
6. Keep pieces no more than one inch thick so nothing gets oversalted. *(paraphrase)*

**Recorded temperature**: **internal ≥160 °F (71 °C)** for a hot-smoked product. This is the only safety-grade number in the leaf, and it is 11–17 °F higher than the Canadian recipe's finishing target.

### 4. Smoked Salmon: Key Temperatures — ThermoWorks blog, citing Harold McGee (United States)
**Inspired by**: https://blog.thermoworks.com/key-temps-smoked-salmon/ (fetched 2026-10-26). ThermoWorks technical blog; the temperature ladder is quoted from Harold McGee, *On Food and Cooking*. **Culinary Group B**.
**Applies to**: smoked fish generally — salmon in the article, and not char-specific

**Facts as published**
1. Hot-smoking takes place at an ambient **150–170 °F (66–77 °C)**, and the fish is smoked until its internal temperature reaches the desired doneness. *(verbatim)*
2. Cold-smoking holds **68–86 °F (20–30 °C)** for 6–12 hours, which leaves the flesh raw and in the danger zone for hours; the article recommends hot smoking instead. *(paraphrase)*
3. McGee's texture ladder for fish: **110 °F** protein begins to shrink and the flesh firms; **120 °F** resilient, less slick, more fibrous; **130 °F** sheets of protein begin to separate and become flaky; **140 °F** firm, fibrous and fragile with little free juice. *(paraphrase)*

**Recorded temperature**: the ambient window **66–77 °C** and the **110–140 °F** texture ladder. Used here for what it explains: the Canadian recipe's 130–140 °F finish is precisely the band where McGee says fish goes from flaky to fragile, i.e. the target is a texture choice, not a safety one.

## Temperature summary

What the collected records actually say, without a consensus being claimed:

| Source | Ambient | Internal target | Product |
|--------|---------|-----------------|---------|
| Bradley Smoker (CA) | 66 → 71 → 77 °C | **54–60 °C** (130–140 °F) | vacuum-sealed, keeping |
| Svenskt Fiske / Olsson (SE) | **60–80 °C** | *none published* | hot dinner, box smoker |
| UAF Extension (US) | — | **≥71 °C** (160 °F) | food-safety requirement |
| ThermoWorks / McGee (US) | **66–77 °C** | 43–60 °C texture ladder | reference |

The one thing that can be said without overreaching: **the only char-specific number in the corpus (54–60 °C) sits below the only safety-grade number in the corpus (71 °C)**, and the safety authority's requirement is not species-specific. Whether Swedish box-smoking at 60–80 °C ambient reliably reaches 71 °C internally in ten minutes is not answered by any source here — and it is the single most useful thing a future pass could find out.

## What makes this method special

Smoking is the one method in this cut where the fat content is the technique rather than a side effect. Char's 5–10 % fat is what lets it take hours of dry heat and salt without turning to leather, which is why the Nordic sources list it first among the fish worth smoking. It is also the method where the corpus is thinnest: a tradition this old, this regional, and this poorly documented in numbers — Swedish home smoking is taught by hand, not by probe.
