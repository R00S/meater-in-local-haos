# Arctic Char × Sous Vide — Recipe Temperature Research

## Cut profile

Sous vide is the only method in this cut where the bath temperature *is* the final temperature, and char
is the case where that property matters most: the published kitchen temperatures for char (42–52 °C) sit
between rare and medium, which is exactly the zone an oven or a pan crosses in under a minute and a water
bath holds indefinitely. Every record here therefore states a **bath temperature**, not a pull
temperature, and the useful question becomes what each bath setting *does to the texture* rather than how
far above or below a target it lands.

The corpus answers that question twice, from opposite ends. The sous vide literature's char chart —
built by the same author who wrote the standard fish tables — maps 40 °C to "slightly warmed" and 60 °C to
"firm and even chewy", with 48.9 °C as "lightly flaky". The restaurant record (Marin, 48 °C) and the
French home record (52 °C) both land inside that map, which is a real cross-check between a
technique reference and independent kitchens. The outlier is equally instructive: the one professional
recipe that uses **72 °C** does so because the fish is about to be **shredded into a brandade and fried**,
i.e. the temperature is chosen to destroy the texture on purpose.

The safety caveat is the one thing every sous vide char source has to say: these temperatures are far
below pasteurisation, so the fish has to be of a quality the cook would eat raw. That is stated by the
chart author explicitly and is implicit in the restaurant and magazine recipes, which both assume
sashimi-grade fish.

## Styckesprofil

Sous vide är den enda metoden i det här snittet där badtemperaturen *är* den färdiga temperaturen, och
röding är fallet där det betyder mest: de publicerade kökstemperaturerna för röding (42–52 °C) ligger
mellan rare och medium, alltså exakt det intervall en ugn eller en panna passerar på under en minut men
som ett vattenbad håller hur länge som helst. Varje källa här anger därför en **badtemperatur**, inte en
temperatur att dra vid.

Korpusen svarar på samma fråga två gånger, från olika håll. Sous vide-litteraturens tabell för röding
mappar 40 °C till "bara uppvärmd" och 60 °C till "fast och till och med seg", med 48,9 °C som "lätt
flikig". Restaurangkällan (Marin, 48 °C) och den franska hemkällan (52 °C) ligger båda inom den kartan,
vilket är en riktig korsverifiering mellan en teknikreferens och oberoende kök. Undantaget är lika
instruktivt: det enda professionella receptet med **72 °C** gör det eftersom fisken ska **mosas till en
brandade och friteras** — temperaturen är vald för att förstöra texturen med avsikt.

Säkerhetsinvändningen är det varje sous vide-källa för röding måste säga: dessa temperaturer ligger långt
under pastörisering, så fisken måste hålla råvarukvalitet.

## Source recipes

### 1. How to Sous Vide Arctic Char — Jason Logsdon, Amazing Food Made Easy (United States)
**Inspired by**: https://www.amazingfoodmadeeasy.com/sous-vide-times-temperatures/how-to-sous-vide/arctic-char (fetched 2026-10-26). Jason Logsdon, author of the *Amazing Food Made Easy* sous vide books and the site's fish temperature tables. **Culinary Group B**.
**Serves**: not stated · **Prep**: optional brine · **Cook**: 25–45 min · **Rest**: none

**Method** (a technique table rather than a single recipe)
1. Arctic char is normally cooked between **104 °F and 140 °F (40 °C and 60 °C)**, which "ranges from just slightly warmed texture up to firm and even chewy at the high end". *(verbatim)*
2. The fish only has to be cooked long enough to heat through, usually **25 to 45 minutes**. *(verbatim)*
3. Brining before cooking helps firm the texture and flavour it; this can be a wet 5 % brine or a dry brine. *(verbatim)*
4. Brine first is "ideal" according to the page summary. *(verbatim)*
5. The table's own texture anchors: **40 °C** slightly warmed · **43.3 °C** firm sashimi · **48.9 °C** lightly flaky and/or firm · **55.5 °C** very flaky and/or firm · **60 °C** chewy. *(verbatim)*
6. Warning printed by the source: the char used "should be high quality fish you would feel comfortable eating raw. The times and temperatures used are almost never enough to pasteurize it." *(verbatim)*

**Recorded temperature**: the **40–60 °C** working range, with 48.9 °C named as the "lightly flaky" point. This is the only record in the cut that gives a texture label for each temperature, and it is the reference the other three are read against.

### 2. Omble chevalier de nos rivières — chef Valentin Marin, Restaurant Lamartine (France)
**Inspired by**: https://www.arts-et-gastronomie.com/recette/omble-chevalier-de-nos-rivieres-champignons-de-la-motte-servolex-et-poivre-de-voatsiperifery/ (fetched 2026-10-26). Arts & Gastronomie, recipe by chef Valentin Marin of Restaurant Lamartine, published 29 June 2023. **Culinary Group B**.
**Serves**: 4 · **Prep**: not stated · **Cook**: ~20 min · **Rest**: none

**Ingredients**
- 4 pavés of arctic char (*omble chevalier*)
- 250 g brown mushrooms, fresh herbs, mushroom powder
- **Consommé**: 500 g brown mushrooms, 500 g water
- **Voatsiperifery pepper butter**: 200 g salted butter, 1 g Voatsiperifery pepper
- **Sauce**: 250 g mushroom consommé, 200 g pepper butter, salt

**Method**
1. Put the mushrooms and water in a sous vide bag, crush the mushrooms, and cook in a steam oven **1 h at 66 °C**; strain through a fine chinois. *(verbatim)*
2. Mix the ground Voatsiperifery pepper into softened salted butter and reserve. *(verbatim)*
3. Combine the mushroom consommé, the pepper butter and salt in a pan and blend. *(verbatim)*
4. **Arctic char**: put the pavés in a sous vide bag and cook them about **20 min in the steam oven at a very low 48 °C**. *(verbatim)*
5. Plate the pavé, cover with finely sliced raw brown mushrooms, dust with mushroom powder, add fresh herbs, and pour the emulsified sauce alongside. *(verbatim)*

**Recorded temperature**: **48 °C for about 20 minutes**, i.e. the "lightly flaky" point on Logsdon's chart, reached by a two-star-standard restaurant kitchen. Note the distinction the recipe itself makes: the consommé is cooked at 66 °C, the fish at 48 °C — the same equipment, two entirely different temperatures, which is the clearest illustration in the corpus of bath temperature as a texture choice.

### 3. Arctic Char Brandade — Sous-Vide Magazine (United States)
**Inspired by**: https://www.sousvidemagazine.com/recipes/fish-seafood/arctic-char-brandade/ (fetched 2026-10-26). Sous-Vide Magazine, an American publication devoted to sous vide cooking; chef-inspired recipe, difficulty "Intermediate". **Culinary Group B**.
**Serves**: 5 · **Prep**: 1 h · **Cook**: 30 min active (excludes sous vide time) · **Rest**: none

**Ingredients**
- **Char**: 1 lb arctic char fillet, 1½ tbsp heavy cream, 2 tbsp kosher salt, 2 garlic cloves, 1 tbsp dried juniper berries, 1 sprig rosemary; 2 eggs and 2 cups breadcrumbs for the coating
- **Potatoes**: 2½ cups Yukon potatoes, 1 cup Idaho potato, 1 tsp kosher salt, 3 garlic cloves, ¼ cup olive oil, 4 tbsp butter
- **Garlic oil**: 1 head garlic, 1 cup olive oil
- **Aquafaba aioli**: 6 tbsp chickpea water, saffron, peppercorns, juniper, 2 cups grapeseed oil, turmeric

**Method**
1. Cook the potatoes in a bag at **181.4 °F / 83 °C for 2 hours**, then crush them. *(verbatim)*
2. Cook garlic and oil at **149 °F / 65 °C for 2 hours**, then strain. *(verbatim)*
3. Salt the fish on both sides, bag it with cream, juniper, garlic and rosemary, and cook at **161.6 °F / 72 °C for 1 hour**. *(verbatim)*
4. Remove the fish from the bag into a mixer, add the potatoes, and blend on slow while drizzling in the garlic oil. *(verbatim)*
5. Scoop into 1-inch balls, egg-wash, coat in breadcrumbs, and deep-fry at **350 °F / 176.6 °C** for up to 3 minutes. *(verbatim)*

**Recorded temperature**: **72 °C for 1 hour** — by far the hottest char bath in the cut, and the record that defines the top of the usable range. It is not a competing opinion about how to serve a char fillet; the fish is deliberately taken past flaky so it can be pulverised and refried. Recorded because a reader scanning bath temperatures for char will find 72 °C in a professional publication and needs to know why.

### 4. Pavé d'omble chevalier à l'oseille, cuisson sous-vide — Cuisine Passion (France)
**Inspired by**: https://www.cuisine-passion.net/recettes/pave-domble-chevalier-a-loseille-cuisson-basse-temperature/ (fetched 2026-10-26). French recipe blog; the page is written in the first person with the author's own tasting verdict. **Culinary Group B**.
**Serves**: 1 · **Prep**: 10 min · **Cook**: 55 min (35 min brine + 20 min bath) · **Rest**: none

**Ingredients**
- 1 pavé of arctic char
- salt and pepper, 1 tbsp olive oil
- a few sorrel leaves, a knob of butter, cream
- a sous vide circulator

**Method**
1. Immerse the pavé in a **10 % salt water solution at 5 °C for 35 minutes**. *(verbatim)*
2. Rinse and dry it well, give it a turn of the pepper mill and just a pinch of salt, and bag it with a spoonful of olive oil. *(verbatim)*
3. Vacuum-seal and cook in the water bath held at a constant **52 °C for 20 minutes**. *(verbatim)*
4. Meanwhile sweat the sorrel in butter, finish with a spoonful of cream and a pinch of salt. *(verbatim)*
5. Open the bag, plate the pavé, and serve with the sorrel sauce and a little ratatouille. *(verbatim)*
6. The author's verdict, recorded because it is a first-hand observation and not a claim: "une texture incomparable, à la fois ferme et fondante", with the intention to go slightly lower next time and to sear the skin side afterwards. *(verbatim)*

**Recorded temperature**: **52 °C for 20 minutes** after a 10 % brine. The brine-then-bath sequence is the only one in the leaf that salts before bagging rather than in the bag, and it is the same 52 °C that the French restaurant record's neighbour (König's oven recipe) treats as the top of the range.

**Source-quality note, recorded honestly**: the page does not display the author's name in the recipe body, so the byline cannot be verified from the page. It is counted because the writing carries clear human markers a template does not produce — a first-person account of receiving the circulator, a stated intention to change the method next time, and a tasting verdict — but a future pass that wants strict name-level attribution should treat this record as weaker than the other three.

## Temperature summary

| Bath temperature | Time | Source | Purpose / texture |
|------------------|------|--------|-------------------|
| 40 °C | 25–45 min | Logsdon (US) | "slightly warmed" — the bottom of the usable range |
| 43.3 °C | 25–45 min | Logsdon (US) | "firm sashimi" |
| **48 °C** | ~20 min | Marin (FR) | lightly flaky, restaurant service |
| 48.9 °C | 25–45 min | Logsdon (US) | "lightly flaky and/or firm" |
| **52 °C** | 20 min | Cuisine Passion (FR) | firm and melting; author plans to go lower |
| 55.5 °C | 25–45 min | Logsdon (US) | "very flaky and/or firm" |
| 60 °C | 25–45 min | Logsdon (US) | "chewy" — the top of the usable range |
| 72 °C | 60 min | Sous-Vide Magazine (US) | deliberately overcooked for a brandade |

**What this supports.** Two independent professional or semi-professional kitchens chose **48 °C and 52 °C**, and both sit inside the texture map published by the technique reference — a genuine cross-check rather than four people repeating one number. The usable window for serving char as a fillet is **48–55 °C**; above 60 °C the fish is described as chewy by the source that measured the ladder, and the only 72 °C recipe exists to destroy the texture on purpose.

**What it does not support.** No record in this leaf gives a *time by thickness*, only "25–45 minutes", so nothing here tells a cook how a 40 mm fillet differs from a 20 mm one. And the safety position is uniform and unresolved: nobody pasteurises char, everybody assumes sashimi-grade fish. That is a real limitation of the method for this cut, not a gap to be filled with a higher number.

## What makes this method special

Char is a fish whose best texture lives in a temperature range that conventional cooking treats as a mistake: 42–52 °C is "raw" to an oven and "perfect" to a char cook, and the difference between the two is a probe and a minute. Sous vide removes the minute. The cost is that it also removes the safety margin — char at 48 °C for 20 minutes has received a fraction of the thermal lethality of a 63 °C oven bake — so the method is inseparable from the quality of the fish. Every source in this leaf says so in some form, and two of them say it outright.

## Data applied to `cooking_data.py`

- `supported_methods` already includes `sous_vide`; no registry change needed for this leaf.
- No `method_temperature_ranges` override. A sous vide range would be a *bath* range, not a serving range, and the engine models serving targets; recording 40–60 °C as a serving band would misstate what the numbers mean. The bath temperatures live in this leaf.
- `recommended_doneness` stays `medium` (54 °C), which is the top of the fillet-serving window this leaf supports.

## Summary

Four records: a technique chart covering **40–60 °C in five labelled texture steps (25–45 min)**, a French restaurant at **48 °C / 20 min**, a French home cook at **52 °C / 20 min**, and one professional **72 °C / 60 min** recipe that only exists because the fish is shredded afterwards. The serving window the corpus supports is **48–55 °C**, the safety caveat is universal (no pasteurisation; sashimi-grade fish required), and no source gives a time-by-thickness.
