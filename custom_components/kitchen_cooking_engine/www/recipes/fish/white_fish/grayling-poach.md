# Grayling × Poach — Recipe Temperature Research

## Cut profile

European grayling (*Thymallus thymallus*; Swedish *harr*) is a small, lean salmonid with fine, fragile flesh. The court-bouillon preparation documented by Mats Sjöstrand gently cooks the whole fish in a strained, seasoned aromatic liquid after it has nearly returned to a boil. The technique suits delicate fish because it avoids direct pan contact and browning; the source gives no internal temperature or measured final endpoint. A whole small fish may have modest carryover, but the recipe does not quantify it and this leaf assigns no numeric carryover value.

## Source recipes

### 1. Mats Sjöstrand / Flugfiskemagasinet Rackelhanen — harr i court-bouillon
**Inspired by**: http://rackelhane.se/swe/1403.htm (fetched 2026-10-23). “Recept på harr,” credited © Mats Sjöstrand 2010; Swedish fishing-magazine recipe and field-cooking guidance.
**Serves**: not stated · **Prep**: broth simmer at least 20 min · **Cook**: gentle simmer until done; duration not stated · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- Grayling, amount not stated
- Water, enough to cover the fish
- 1 tbsp vinegar or wine
- 1 white onion, size not stated
- 1 piece of carrot, size not stated
- 1 bunch of parsley, size not stated
- A pinch of thyme
- A few teaspoons of salt
- A few peppercorns, added near the end of broth preparation

**Method**
1. Combine water, vinegar or wine, onion, carrot, parsley, thyme and salt; add the peppercorns near the end.
2. Boil the aromatic broth for at least 20 minutes, then strain it.
3. Bring the strained court-bouillon to a boil and add the grayling.
4. Let the liquid nearly return to a boil, skim it, then lower the heat and simmer the fish gently until cooked. The source does not state a duration, internal temperature, or explicit pull point.
5. For a larger fish intended to be served cold, cool it in the broth. The source does not give a final serving temperature.

## What makes this method special

Poaching cooks grayling in a gently moving aromatic liquid rather than by direct dry heat, limiting browning while seasoning the flesh. It is a suitable way to preserve the fish's delicate texture and, in the source's larger-fish variant, the fish cools in its broth for service cold.
