# Sea Bass × Grill — Recipe Temperature Research

## Cut profile

Grilling cooks sea bass whole, and that changes the physics completely from the pan: the
fish is heated from outside in on both faces at once, the skin tightens and blisters rather
than sitting flat against metal, and the cavity traps the aromatics that are stuffed into it.
The two failures are specific — the skin welds itself to the grate before it sets, and the
belly, which is far thinner than the shoulder, dries before the thickest part is done. All
three fetched sources answer the first one the same way: **do not move the fish until it
releases by itself**, which is simultaneously the doneness signal. Two of them grill over
charcoal (*mangal* in the Turkish source, *κάρβουνα* in the Greek one) and one over a high
gas or indoor grill; the Turkish source withholds salt until the surface has taken colour and
the Greek one bastes every time the grate is turned. None publishes an internal temperature —
whole-fish grilling in all three traditions is judged by the fish coming free of the grate,
by the eye turning white, and by time per side. Probe through the shoulder behind the head,
which is the thickest point of a whole sea bass.

## Styckesprofil

Grillning tillagar havsabborren hel, och det ändrar fysiken helt jämfört med pannan: fisken
hettas upp utifrån och in från båda sidor samtidigt, skinnet spänns och blåsor bildas i
stället för att ligga plant mot metall, och bukhålan håller kvar de aromater som fyllts i den.
Två typiska fel: skinnet fastnar i gallret innan det stelnat, och buken som är mycket tunnare
än bogen torkar ut innan den tjockaste delen är klar. Alla tre hämtade källor löser det första
på samma sätt: **rör inte fisken förrän den släpper av sig själv**, vilket samtidigt är
klarhets-tecknet. Två grillar över kol (*mangal* hos den turkiska källan, *κάρβουνα* hos den
grekiska), en över het gasol- eller inomhusgrill. Ingen publicerar innertemperatur — hel fisk
bedöms efter att den släpper från gallret, att ögat vitnar och efter tid per sida. Sond genom
bogen bakom huvudet.

## Source recipes

### 1. Little Ferraro Kitchen — Grilled Branzino with Lemon and Fresh Herbs (Samantha Ferraro, US)
**Inspired by**: https://littleferrarokitchen.com/grilled-branzino-lemon-fresh-herbs/ (fetched 2026-10-26). Samantha Ferraro, Little Ferraro Kitchen (US, Italian/Mediterranean tradition).
**Serves**: 4 · **Prep**: 10 min · **Cook**: 15 min · **Rest**: "while the fish rests" (lemon grills), duration not stated

**Ingredients**
- 2 whole branzino (European sea bass), about 1½ lb each
- 1 lemon, half sliced for the cavity, half saved for grilling
- A few sprigs of fresh parsley, cilantro, thyme or basil
- 1 tsp kosher salt; ½ tsp ground black pepper
- 2–3 tbsp olive oil
- **Herb olive oil**: ¼ cup olive oil; ½ lemon, zested and juiced; ¼ cup parsley, basil or cilantro, finely chopped; salt and pepper to taste

**Method**
1. Have the fishmonger clean, de-scale and gut the branzino; pat the outside completely dry.
2. Preheat an **outdoor or indoor grill to high**. Season the cavity with salt and pepper and stuff it with 2–3 lemon slices and a few herb sprigs.
3. Brush olive oil liberally over the whole fish and lay it on the hot grate. Cook **5–7 minutes on the first side**, until the skin is charred.
4. Carefully flip with a flexible fish spatula and cook **another 5 minutes**, until the fish looks opaque. Thicker fish may need an **extra 2–3 minutes**.
5. Rest briefly, then open the fish along the back, lift out the spine and head, and spoon the herb oil over the fillets.
6. The source publishes **no internal temperature**; its cue is charred skin plus opaque flesh. House guidance (not the source's): probe through the shoulder behind the head and **pull at 60 °C (140 °F)**, carrying to the 63 °C (145 °F) USDA line while the fish rests. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.

### 2. Mutfak Sırları — Izgara Levrek (Turkish, over *mangal* coals)
**Inspired by**: https://mutfaksirlari.com/izgara-levrek.html (fetched 2026-10-26). Mutfak Sırları (Turkish home-cooking publication; the recipe is explicitly a *mangal* cook over embers).
**Serves**: 2 · **Prep**: 10 min (+ 15–20 min oil rest) · **Cook**: 15 min · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 1 sea bass (*levrek*) per person, about 550–600 g
- Olive oil (enough to coat the fish inside and out)
- Salt; freshly ground black pepper
- **To serve**: lemon slices; red onion slices; tomato and curly lettuce, optional

**Method**
1. Clean the fish, wash it and dry the inside and outside thoroughly with paper towel.
2. Put the fish in a bowl, pour plenty of olive oil over it and rub it inside and out; **rest 15–20 minutes** (garlic, bay leaf or rosemary can go into the oil if wanted).
3. Let the fire burn down from flame to **glowing embers (*köz*)** — the source is explicit that a flaming fire burns the outside and leaves the inside raw — then put the grate on and let it heat through.
4. Lay the drained fish on the hot grate and cook on the first face **until grill marks appear and the fish releases from the grate by itself**. The source's rule: *"ayrılmıyorsa tam pişmemiş demektir, ellemeyin"* — if it does not come away, it is not ready; do not touch it.
5. Turn and cook the second face the same way; serve hot with lemon and onion.
6. The source publishes **no internal temperature** and no clock beyond the 15-minute total; the release-from-grate moment is its doneness signal. House guidance (not the source's): probe through the shoulder and **pull at 60 °C (140 °F)**. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.

### 3. Cookpad Greece — Λαβράκια στα κάρβουνα (Panagiotis L., Zakynthos)
**Inspired by**: https://cookpad.com/gr/sintages/8293722 (fetched 2026-10-26). Panagiotis L., Cookpad Greece (home cook, Zakynthos; charcoal/wood-fire cook, Palm Sunday fish tradition).
**Serves**: 2 · **Prep**: 45 min (fire lighting; the source counts it) · **Cook**: 15–20 min · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 2 sea bass (*λαβράκια*)
- 1 lemon
- 3–4 sprigs fresh oregano
- 100 g oil
- Salt, pepper, dried oregano

**Method**
1. Clean and wash the fish, dry them, season with salt and pepper and put **1–2 oregano sprigs inside each belly**.
2. In a bowl squeeze the lemon and add the oil, salt, pepper and a little oregano to make a *ladolemono*.
3. Light the charcoal; when it is ready, oil the fish lightly and lay them on the grate. Because the fire is fierce at the start, **turn the grate every 2–3 minutes and oil each time**.
4. Continue for **15–20 minutes** depending on the size of the fish, then lift onto a platter and pour the remaining *ladolemono* over them. Serve with salad or wild greens.
5. The source publishes **no internal temperature**; doneness is time plus the fish holding together on the grate. House guidance (not the source's): probe through the shoulder and **pull at 60 °C (140 °F)**. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.

## Temperature consensus

| Source | Published pull / doneness signal | Tradition and fuel |
|--------|--------------------------------|--------------------|
| Little Ferraro Kitchen (US) | No temperature; charred skin and opaque flesh; 5–7 min + 5 min | Italian-Mediterranean whole fish, high gas or indoor grill |
| Mutfak Sırları (Turkey) | No temperature; fish must release from the grate unaided; ~15 min | Turkish *mangal*, glowing charcoal embers |
| Cookpad Greece (Greece) | No temperature; 15–20 min, grate turned every 2–3 min and re-oiled | Greek *κάρβουνα*, charcoal, *ladolemono* finish |

The finding here is a negative one and it is consistent across three traditions: **no
fetched grilling source for this cut publishes an internal temperature**, so the leaf's
observed range is empty and no `method_temperature_ranges` override can be justified from
evidence. What the sources substitute is a **mechanical** doneness test — the fish releasing
from the grate — which is in fact a better signal than time for a whole fish of unknown
thickness, and the Turkish source states it as a rule ("if it does not come away, it is not
ready; do not touch it"). Times cluster at **15 minutes total** (US 5–7 + 5, Turkey ~15,
Greece 15–20), i.e. roughly 7 minutes per side for a 550–600 g fish, which is the weight
band both the Turkish and Greek sources specify. Mapping to the engine: the 60 °C (140 °F)
pull quoted in the method lines is **house guidance (see the cut's `oven_roast` leaf for the
one fetched source that does publish 145 °F)**; `recommended_doneness` for this method stays
**`well_done`**, since all three sources cook to opaque flesh and none describes a rare
whole grilled fish.

## What makes this method special

A grill is a radiant heat source with a grate in the middle of it, and whole sea bass
responds to that in a way a fillet cannot: the cavity becomes a humid chamber, so the inside
of the belly steams while the outside blisters, and the fish effectively cooks by two
mechanisms at once. The skin is the vulnerable part — it goes from slack to glued within the
first minute and only releases once the proteins beneath it have set and contracted, which is
why every tradition here encodes "leave it alone" as the technique rather than a temperature.
Charcoal adds the second variable the gas source does not have: embers give a steady, even
heat, while flame gives a violent one that chars the outside before the shoulder is warm,
and the Turkish source makes that the central instruction. The Greek habit of turning the
grate rather than the fish spreads the load across both faces without risking torn skin, and
the oil layer — applied before grilling and again at each turn — is doing double duty as
release agent and heat-transfer medium.

## Summary

Grilled whole sea bass is a fifteen-minute cook over high, steady heat — 5 to 7 minutes on
the first side, about 5 on the second, over charcoal embers or a hot gas grate. The skin is
what decides success: it welds to the grate until the flesh beneath it sets, so the fish is
turned only when it releases on its own, and that release is also the best doneness signal
you have. Stuff the belly, oil the skin at every turn, and keep the fire at ember stage
rather than flame so the outside does not char before the shoulder is cooked. Excellent
match for the cut; the one thing it will not give you is a number — none of these traditions
probes.
