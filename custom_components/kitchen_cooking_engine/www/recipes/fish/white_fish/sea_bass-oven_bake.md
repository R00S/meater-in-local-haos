# Sea Bass × Oven Bake — Recipe Temperature Research

## Cut profile

Baking a whole sea bass inside a crust of coarse salt is the Mediterranean's most
protective way to cook this fish, and it is genuinely a different method from roasting: the
salt, dampened with egg white or water, sets into a sealed shell that traps the fish's own
vapour, so the flesh effectively steams while the skin is shielded from the dry oven air and
from the salt itself. The one non-negotiable rule all three fetched sources share is
**do not scale the fish** — the scales are the barrier that stops the salt from drawing
moisture out. What they disagree about is the clock, and by a lot: the Italian source bakes
a 500 g fish at **220 °C for 25 minutes** and adds **15 minutes per extra 500 g**, while the
Spanish source gives **28 minutes for 1.5 kg** on a weight table (20 min for 1 kg, 35 min for
2 kg, 45 min for 3 kg) without stating an oven temperature at all, and the Russian source
gives **about 35 minutes** for a single fish with no temperature either. That is not a
contradiction to resolve — it is a temperature difference (220 °C versus an unstated, almost
certainly lower setting) showing up as a time difference. Because the fish is sealed, there
is no probe access that is worth anything: the Spanish tail-pull test is the only real
doneness signal any of the three offers.

## Source recipes

### 1. Cookaround — Branzino al sale (Italy)
**Inspired by**: https://www.cookaround.com/ricetta/Branzino-al-sale.html (fetched 2026-10-26). Cookaround (Italian recipe publication).
**Serves**: 4 · **Prep**: 10 min · **Cook**: 25 min · **Rest**: "a few minutes" before breaking the crust

**Ingredients**
- 2 kg coarse salt (*sale grosso*)
- 2 branzino (European sea bass), 500 g each, **gutted but not scaled**
- 2 lemons, sliced
- 5 parsley leaves

**Method**
1. Cut two lemon slices and halve them; chop the parsley roughly and work it with a pinch of salt; rub the lemon slices with the parsley salt.
2. Spread **a quarter of the coarse salt** over the bottom of a tray large enough to hold the fish. Lay the cleaned, **unscaled** fish on it, put two lemon slices inside the belly and close the flaps.
3. Cover the fish completely with the remaining salt — add more if needed. Bake in a **static oven preheated to 220 °C for 25 minutes**. The source's scaling rule, verbatim: *"per ogni 500 g. di pesce in più, sarà necessario aggiungere 15 minuti di cottura"* — 15 extra minutes per additional 500 g.
4. Wait a few minutes, then break the salt away with a spoon and brush off the remainder; the skin comes away easily.
5. The source's doneness test: *"Se la pelle viene via facilmente, e la carne risulta umida e succosa, allora il pesce è cotto alla perfezione"* — if the skin lifts easily and the flesh is moist, it is perfectly cooked; otherwise return it to the oven for a few minutes uncovered. It publishes **no internal temperature**. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source** (served with oil and lemon straight from the crust).

### 2. Directo al Paladar — Cómo hacer la lubina a la sal (Pakus, Spain)
**Inspired by**: https://www.directoalpaladar.com/recetas-de-pescados-y-mariscos/como-hacer-la-lubina-a-la-sal-y-dos-trucos-para-que-salga-perfecta (fetched 2026-10-26). Pakus (Alfonso López), Directo al Paladar (Spanish food publication).
**Serves**: 4 · **Prep**: not stated · **Cook**: 30 min · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 1 large sea bass (*lubina*), 1.5 kg
- 2 kg coarse salt
- 1 egg white

**Method**
1. Beat the egg white and work it through the coarse salt so the salt becomes manageable and clings to the fish without crumbling.
2. Spread a **half-centimetre bed of salt** on a parchment-lined tray, lay the fish on its side on it, then cover it handful by handful with the rest of the salt, pressing lightly. The fishmonger must **clean it without opening it and leave the scales on**.
3. **Leave the tail outside the salt** — the source's first trick, because there is no way to probe a salt-baked fish: if the tail comes away when pulled, the fish is done; if it holds, it needs longer.
4. **Second trick**: score the silhouette of the fish into the salt with a knife before baking, so the crust lifts off in one piece afterwards.
5. The source's weight table: **1 kg → 20 min; 1.5 kg → 28 min; 2 kg → 35 min; 2.5 kg → 40 min; 3 kg → 45 min.** The recipe body does not state an oven temperature.
6. It publishes **no internal temperature**; the tail test is the doneness test. House guidance (not the source's): if you want a number, a 1.5 kg fish at 220 °C on the Italian schedule would be about 45 minutes, so the Spanish 28 minutes implies a gentler oven — set 180–200 °C and trust the tail. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.

### 3. Povar.ru — Сибас, запечённый в соли (Marina Zolottseva, Russia)
**Inspired by**: https://m.povar.ru/recipes/sibas_zapechennyi_v_soli-27867.html (fetched 2026-10-26). Marina Zolottseva, Povar.ru (Russian recipe publication).
**Serves**: not stated (1 fish) · **Prep**: not stated · **Cook**: about 35 min · **Rest**: 5 min

**Ingredients**
- 1 whole sea bass (*сибас*), gutted and dried
- 2 kg salt
- 1 orange
- 1 lemon or lime
- 2 egg whites
- Parsley, to taste

**Method**
1. Wash the fish, gut it and dry it with paper towel; wash and dry the citrus.
2. Mix the salt with the egg whites and a little water so it is damp enough to hold together — the source compares the texture to wet sand.
3. Line a tray with parchment, spread about half the salt, lay the fish on it and put the aromatics **inside the belly** (the source's combination is sour lime, sweet orange and parsley).
4. Cover the fish with the remaining salt, pressing it on firmly; **the head may be left uncovered**.
5. Bake for **about 35 minutes**. The source states no oven temperature.
6. Leave the fish **5 minutes** out of the oven to "rest", then break off the salt, remove the skin and serve in portions.
7. It publishes **no internal temperature**; doneness is the 35 minutes plus the rest. House guidance (not the source's): an unstated oven in a Russian home recipe is normally 180–200 °C; probe is impractical through the crust, so lift a small patch of salt at the shoulder and **check for 60–63 °C (140–145 °F)** if you want confirmation. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.

## Temperature consensus

| Source | Published pull / doneness signal | Tradition, oven setting and time |
|--------|--------------------------------|----------------------------------|
| Cookaround (Italy) | No temperature; skin lifts easily and flesh is moist | 220 °C static, 25 min for 500 g + 15 min per extra 500 g |
| Directo al Paladar (Spain) | No temperature; **tail-pull test** | Oven temperature not stated; 1 kg 20 min, 1.5 kg 28 min, 2 kg 35 min, 2.5 kg 40 min, 3 kg 45 min |
| Povar.ru (Russia) | No temperature; 35 min + 5 min rest | Oven temperature not stated; about 35 min for one fish |

**No source publishes an internal temperature**, and two of the three do not even publish an
oven temperature — so the numeric spread this leaf can honestly offer is a *time* spread,
not a temperature one: **20 to 45 minutes** across a 0.5–3 kg weight range, with the Italian
source as the only one that fixes its oven (220 °C) and therefore the only one whose times
can be read as a rate. Read that way, the Italian 25 min/500 g and the Spanish 28 min/1.5 kg
imply materially different oven settings, which is exactly what the missing Spanish figure
would have told us; the leaf records the divergence as the finding rather than picking a
winner. Mapping to the engine: the crust makes a probe awkward but not impossible, so the
house-guidance line is **60–63 °C (140–145 °F)** at the shoulder, which is the cut's
`FISH_WELL_DONE` band and the USDA floor; `recommended_doneness` for this method:
**`well_done`** — all three sources describe a fully set, moist-fleshed fish, and none of them
is aiming at translucency.

## What makes this method special

The salt crust is a vessel, not a seasoning: mixed with egg white it sets into a rigid shell
that seals the fish inside its own atmosphere, so heat arrives by conduction through the salt
and the fish cooks in saturated vapour rather than dry air. That is why the flesh comes out
noticeably moister than a roasted fish of the same size and why the skin, protected from the
oven and from the salt by the scales, lifts away in one piece. The shell also buffers the
heat: the outside of the crust can be far hotter than the fish, because the salt's mass and
the evaporating moisture inside hold the interface near 100 °C, which is what makes a
twenty-minute tolerance possible. The cost is control — you cannot see, probe or baste
anything — so every tradition invents a proxy: the Spanish tail-pull, the Italian
lift-the-skin test, the Russian rest before breaking. It is also the only method here that
would actively ruin the fish if the scales were removed first.

## Summary

Baking sea bass in a crust of coarse salt cooks it in its own trapped vapour, which is why the
flesh comes out moister than a plain roast and the skin lifts off in one piece. It only works
if the fish is left unscaled — the scales are what keep the salt from drawing the moisture
out. Times run from 20 minutes for a kilo to 45 for three, and the traditions disagree widely
because most of them never state their oven temperature. You cannot probe it easily, so use
the tail-pull test or check at the shoulder through a small opening. A forgiving, dramatic
method for a whole fish; poor value for a fillet.
