# Sea Bass × Steam — Recipe Temperature Research

## Cut profile

Steaming is the East Asian answer to this fish, and it is the gentlest heat the cut ever
meets: the flesh sits in vapour at around 100 °C with no contact surface, so there is no
gradient to manage, no skin to crisp, and almost nothing to lose to the pan. What the method
demands instead is **timing and draining**. All three fetched sources are within a few
minutes of each other for a whole fish of roughly 500–700 g — the Thai source gives 8–12
minutes over fully boiling water, the Chinese source 10 minutes for a large fish and 7–8 for
a 500 g one plus 5 minutes off the heat, and the Japanese source 6 minutes in a microwave
steamer for two cuts — and two of the three make **pouring off the collected juices** the
step that decides whether the dish tastes clean. The fish itself is the other variable: the
Thai recipe is written for barramundi/Asian sea bass, the Chinese for *lǜyú* (the sea-bass
name used across the Chinese-speaking world), and the Japanese for *suzuki*, so this leaf
documents the tradition rather than a single species. Probe through the shoulder behind the
head — the thickest point — and expect the belly to be well past it.

## Source recipes

### 1. Hot Thai Kitchen — Pla Gapong Neung Manao (Pailin Chongchitnant with Mark Wiens, Thailand)
**Inspired by**: https://hot-thai-kitchen.com/mark-wiens-pla-gapong-neung-manao/ (fetched 2026-10-26). Pailin Chongchitnant, Hot Thai Kitchen (Thai chef; recipe filmed with Mark Wiens). Fish named by the source: barramundi / Asian sea bass, with branzino or sea bass given as substitutes.
**Serves**: 4 · **Prep**: 15 min · **Cook**: 15 min · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 1.5 lb (about 680 g) whole barramundi or another white fish, guts and gills removed
- 1 stalk lemongrass, bottom half only, smashed and cut in chunks
- ½ cup good chicken or fish stock
- 1 tbsp finely chopped palm sugar
- 4 tbsp lime juice
- 3 tbsp fish sauce
- 1 head garlic, chopped
- Thai chillies, to taste, finely chopped
- 8–10 sprigs cilantro, chopped
- 1 stalk Chinese celery, cut into 1-inch pieces

**Method**
1. **Score the fish** with 3 diagonal incisions on each side, making the cuts on the two sides perpendicular to each other. Stuff the belly with the lemongrass and set the fish on a plate.
2. Steam over **fully boiling water for about 8–12 minutes**, depending on the size of the fish (the source notes 20 minutes for the very large fish used in the video).
3. Meanwhile heat the stock and palm sugar until the sugar dissolves; pour into a bowl and stir in the garlic, chillies, fish sauce and lime juice. Taste and adjust.
4. Lift the fish onto a platter, scatter Chinese celery leaves around it, stir the cilantro into the sauce and pour it over the fish.
5. The source publishes **no internal temperature**; doneness is the 8–12 minutes over boiling water. House guidance (not the source's): probe through the shoulder and **pull at 60 °C (140 °F)** — steamed fish keeps climbing in its own heat while the sauce is poured. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.

### 2. Xiachufang — 清蒸鲈鱼 (steamed sea bass, 木可厨房, China)
**Inspired by**: https://m.xiachufang.com/recipe/264051/ (fetched 2026-10-26). 木可厨房, Xiachufang (Chinese home-cooking community). ⚠️ The page lists its ingredients **without quantities** — the source's own list is a bare enumeration (*sea bass, green garlic, ginger, lard, steamed-fish soy*), and it is reproduced here as published rather than filled in from guesswork.
**Serves**: not stated · **Prep**: 10 min salting · **Cook**: about 10 min + 5 min off the heat · **Rest**: the 5 minutes off the heat is the rest

**Ingredients** (as listed by the source; no amounts published)
- 鲈鱼 sea bass (*lǜyú*), whole, cleaned
- 青蒜 green garlic/leek
- 姜 ginger
- 猪油 lard (the source prefers it to salad oil for the finish)
- 蒸鱼豉油 steamed-fish soy sauce

**Method**
1. Clean and dry the fish, make a few diagonal cuts on both sides, rub a little salt over the skin and inside, and **marinate 10 minutes or more** — the source warns to use less salt than you think because the soy goes on at the end.
2. Stuff ginger and garlic in the belly, scatter more over the fish, add a splash of cooking wine, and put it into the steamer **once the water is at a rolling boil**. Steam **about 10 minutes**; the source's own note: *"if it is a fish of about 500 g, only 7–8 minutes"* — heat is critical, too short and it is raw, too long and the flesh goes tough.
3. **Turn off the heat and leave it covered for a further 5 minutes** (*虚蒸*) rather than opening the lid, so the fish finishes gently.
4. Remove the cooked ginger and garlic and **pour off all the liquid in the plate** — the source calls this the step that removes the fishiness.
5. Lay fresh ginger and garlic shreds on the fish, pour a spoonful of **hot lard** over them (so the aromatics bloom), then finish with steamed-fish soy sauce at the table.
6. The source publishes **no internal temperature**; doneness is the 10 + 5 minute schedule. House guidance (not the source's): probe through the shoulder before the covered rest and **pull at 58 °C (136 °F)**, since the 5 covered minutes will carry it to the 63 °C (145 °F) line. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.

### 3. Cookpad Japan — スズキの酒蒸し (sake-steamed sea bass, Japan)
**Inspired by**: https://cookpad.com/jp/recipes/19788232 (fetched 2026-10-26). Cookpad user クック3TGLV1 (Japanese home cook and angler). Fish: *suzuki* (Japanese sea bass). ⚠️ The source publishes its seasonings as *少々* ("a little") — recorded as published, not converted into grams.
**Serves**: not stated (2 cuts of fish) · **Prep**: not stated · **Cook**: about 6 min · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 2 cuts of suzuki (sea bass)
- ½ long onion (*negi*)
- 2 slices ginger
- A little sake
- A little salt

**Method**
1. Cut the ingredients "to a sensible size".
2. Put the fish, sake and salt into a heatproof bowl and rub them together lightly.
3. **Cover the bowl with plastic film and microwave for about 6 minutes.**
4. Plate and serve; the source suggests grated daikon and ponzu if it tastes flat.
5. The source publishes **no internal temperature** and no visual doneness cue beyond the 6-minute microwave time — the method's control is the wattage of the appliance, not a probe. House guidance (not the source's): with a domestic 600–800 W microwave, check at 5 minutes and **pull at 58–60 °C (136–140 °F)**. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.

## Temperature consensus

| Source | Published pull / doneness signal | Tradition, vessel and time |
|--------|--------------------------------|----------------------------|
| Hot Thai Kitchen (Thailand) | No temperature; 8–12 min over fully boiling water | Bamboo/metal steamer, scored whole fish, lime-garlic sauce poured after |
| Xiachufang (China) | No temperature; ~10 min (7–8 min for 500 g) + 5 min covered off the heat | Wok/steamer over rolling boil, juices poured off, hot lard and soy finish |
| Cookpad Japan (Japan) | No temperature; about 6 min | Microwave steamer, sake and salt, no sauce |

**No fetched steaming source publishes an internal temperature.** That is a clean finding
rather than a gap in this pass: steaming at 100 °C is self-limiting in the sense that the
flesh cannot exceed the vapour temperature, so every tradition here controls the result with
time and with the **covered rest** instead. The three times line up once the form is
accounted for — 7–8 minutes for a 500 g whole fish (Chinese), 8–12 minutes for a 680 g whole
fish (Thai), 6 minutes for two bone-in cuts in a microwave (Japanese) — and the Chinese
source's extra 5 minutes off the heat is functionally the same idea as the Thai and Japanese
sources' brief stand before saucing. Mapping to the engine: the 58–60 °C pulls written into
the method lines are **house guidance**, chosen so the covered rest lands the centre on the
63 °C (145 °F) USDA line; `recommended_doneness` for this method stays **`well_done`**, since
no tradition here serves steamed sea bass translucent.

## What makes this method special

Steam is the one cooking medium that cannot overshoot: as long as there is vapour, the surface
of the fish sits at about 100 °C and the only way to overcook is to leave it there too long.
That flattens the gradient completely — there is no hot face and cool centre as in a pan, just
a slow, even advance inward — which is why a whole fish can be steamed with a five-minute
tolerance where a seared fillet has three. The other thing steam does is **dilute**: condensed
water collects on the plate carrying dissolved protein and fishy volatiles, and the Chinese and
Thai sources both treat removing it as essential rather than incidental. Flavour therefore
arrives after the cooking, not during it: hot lard or hot oil poured over raw ginger and
scallion, or a lime-garlic sauce stirred together while the fish steams. The cost is skin —
steamed sea bass skin is slack and pale, not crisp, so this is the method for people who want
the flesh and not the crust.

## Summary

Steaming is the gentlest way to cook this fish and the most forgiving of a whole one: 8 to 12
minutes over boiling water for a fish around 700 g, or 7 to 8 minutes for 500 g, then a few
minutes covered off the heat. It cannot overshoot the way a pan can, but it also cannot crisp
the skin — what you get is pure, barely set flesh that takes its flavour afterwards from hot
aromatics or a sharp lime-garlic sauce. Pour off the liquid that collects on the plate; that
step, more than any timing tweak, is what separates a clean steamed fish from a fishy one.
Best when the fish is very fresh and you want to taste it rather than the cooking.
