# Swordfish × Sous Vide — Recipe Temperature Research

## Cut profile

Sous vide holds swordfish at one exact temperature from edge to centre for 20–45 minutes,
then most sources finish with a 1–2 minute sear for surface flavour. The fetched sources
form a tight cluster at 54.4–55.5 °C (130–132 °F) — the American consensus for "medium,
firm but juicy" — with two deliberate outliers: a German home cook at 59 °C for 20 minutes
followed by a hard cast-iron crisping, and an American source bathing at 49 °C (120 °F)
for 45 minutes for "lightly flaky and firm". Swordfish is a good sous vide subject precisely
because its dense, uniform flesh has no flaking layers to fall apart during the long hold,
and because its overcook failure mode (chalkiness above ~60 °C) is exactly what a
temperature-locked bath eliminates. The wet-bag texture before searing is the trade: no
crust unless you add one afterwards.

## Styckesprofil

Sous vide håller svärdisk på exakt en temperatur kant till kant i 20–45 minuter; de flesta
källorna avslutar med 1–2 minuters stekning för ytsmak. Hämtade källor klusterar vid
54,4–55,5 °C (amerikansk konsensus om medium, saftig), med två avvikare: en tysk hemmakok
på 59 °C i 20 minuter följd av hård stekning i gjutjärn, och en amerikansk källa på 49 °C
i 45 minuter. Svärdisk passar metoden eftersom det täta köttet håller ihop under det långa
badet och eftersom överkokningsrisken (kalligt över ~60 °C) är exakt vad ett temperaturlåst
bad eliminerar. Kompromissen är ytan: ingen skorpa utan efterföljande stekning.

## Source recipes

### 1. Anova Culinary (Emily Farris & Jeff Akin) — Sous Vide Swordfish
**Inspired by**: https://recipes.anovaculinary.com/recipe/sous-vide-swordfish-1 (fetched 2026-10-25). Anova Culinary (US precision-cooker maker); 929 ratings.
**Serves**: 2 · **Prep**: 15 min · **Cook**: 30 min (up to 45) + 1–2 min sear · **Rest**: 5 min

**Ingredients**
- 2 (6-ounce) swordfish steaks
- 2 tbsp extra-virgin olive oil; zest and juice of 2 lemons; 4 sprigs fresh thyme; kosher salt and black pepper

**Method**
1. Set the sous vide bath to **130 °F (54.4 °C)** — the source's stated recipe temperature (page text rounds to 55 °C).
2. Season the fish with salt and pepper; bag with the olive oil, lemon zest, lemon juice and thyme; seal by water displacement or vacuum (moist setting).
3. Cook **30 minutes, or up to 45**.
4. Remove the bag; pat the swordfish very dry and reserve the cooking liquid.
5. Heat a grill, grill pan or cast-iron skillet to high (600–700 °F); sear **1–2 minutes per side**.
6. **Pull at 54 °C (130 °F)** — bath temperature is the pull temperature; rest 5 minutes. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source** (rest + residual sear heat raise it).

### 2. Anova Culinary (Chef Jen Carroll, Philadelphia) — Olive Oil Sous Vide Swordfish
**Inspired by**: https://recipes.anovaculinary.com/recipe/olive-oil-sous-vide-swordfish (fetched 2026-10-25). Anova Culinary; recipe by Top Chef's Jen Carroll, framed as "very similar to an olive oil poach but much more controlled & exact".
**Serves**: 2 · **Prep**: 10 min · **Cook**: 30 min (+5–10 for thicker) + ≤1 min sear · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 2 × 8 oz swordfish steaks (1.5 in thick)
- Kosher salt; black pepper; ¼ bunch (3 g) thyme, chopped; 1 (3 g) garlic clove, minced; zest of ½ lemon; 3½ tbsp (28 g) olive oil

**Method**
1. Fill the bath and set it to **130 °F (54.4 °C)**.
2. Season the steaks with salt and pepper; mix the garlic, parsley/thyme, lemon zest and olive oil; spoon 1 tbsp of the herb oil into each vacuum bag with a steak and seal (vacuum "moist" setting; hang the bag so liquid stays out of the chamber).
3. When the bath reaches temperature, cook: a 1.5-inch steak takes **about 30 minutes**; add 5–10 minutes for thicker cuts.
4. Remove; pat dry with a lint-free towel and discard the bag liquid.
5. The fish is now cooked and ready; for surface flavour, lightly oil a medium-high pan and sear **up to 1 minute, presentation side only**. **Pull at 54 °C (130 °F)** — the source's bath temperature. Finish with a squeeze of lemon. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.

### 3. Amazing Food Made Easy (Jason Logsdon) — Sous Vide Swordfish
**Inspired by**: https://www.amazingfoodmadeeasy.com/info/modernist-recipes/more/sous-vide-swordfish-recipe (fetched 2026-10-25). Modernist cooking site; author of ten sous vide cookbooks.
**Serves**: 2 · **Prep**: 15 min · **Cook**: 15–30 min · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 1 pound swordfish, either chunks or a whole fillet
- 2 fresh sprigs thyme; ½–1 tbsp lemon verbena, diced (optional); 1 tsp fresh mint, diced; 2 tbsp butter; salt and pepper

**Method**
1. Season the swordfish with salt and pepper; bag with the thyme, lemon verbena, mint and butter; seal.
2. Preheat the water bath to **132 °F (55.5 °C)** — the source's stated temperature.
3. Cook **15 to 30 minutes** (the source notes the range exists for all sous vide timings).
4. Remove; reserve the butter-herb liquid. For finishing, pat dry and sear over high heat in a hot pan or on a grill — or pour the butter-herb liquid over as a sauce without searing.
5. **Pull at 55.5 °C (132 °F)** — bath temperature. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.

### 4. Went Here 8 This — Sous Vide Swordfish with Grapefruit and Shallot Sauce
**Inspired by**: https://www.wenthere8this.com/sous-vide-swordfish/ (fetched 2026-10-25). American food blog "Went Here 8 This".
**Serves**: not stated (2 pieces) · **Prep**: not stated · **Cook**: 45 min · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 2 swordfish steaks, ideally same thickness (1–1½ in); salt and pepper
- **Grapefruit shallot sauce**: 3 tbsp unsalted butter; ¼ cup grapefruit juice; 3 sprigs fresh thyme; 1 shallot, thinly sliced; ½ tsp salt

**Method**
1. Heat a sous vide water bath to **120 °F (49 °C)** — the source's stated temperature for "lightly flaky and firm" steaks; its FAQ raises the bath to **132 °F (55.5 °C)** for "really firm and flaky".
2. Season the swordfish with salt and pepper; vacuum seal in a single layer, fully submerged (use a sinker weight if it floats).
3. Cook **45 minutes**; remove and pat dry.
4. Melt the butter over medium heat; soften the shallots and thyme 3–5 minutes; add the grapefruit juice and simmer 2–3 minutes.
5. Serve the fish topped with the sauce and grapefruit chunks. **Pull at 49 °C (120 °F)** — the source's bath temperature (55.5 °C / 132 °F variant noted above). Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.

### 5. eatdrinkcooking (toko) — crispy swordfish – krosser Schwertfisch, sous-vide
**Inspired by**: http://eatdrinkcooking.blogspot.com/2015/03/crispy-swordfish-krosser-schwertfisch.html (fetched 2026-10-25). German home-cooking blog (German-language recipe, two-phase sous vide + cast-iron finish).
**Serves**: 4 · **Prep**: ~1 h marinating + vegetable prep · **Cook**: 20 min bath + 80 s sear · **Rest**: 2 min ice-water stop

**Ingredients**
- 320 g swordfish, 4 fillets, 2.5–3 cm thick
- Very good olive oil, splash of lemon juice, chopped rosemary, sea salt, black pepper (marinade)
- 1 aubergine; 2 bulbs fresh garlic; 4 baby artichokes; juice of ½ lemon; 1 handful root spinach; 1 handful red baby chard; 2 tbsp pine nuts; 1 bunch thyme; 2 sprigs rosemary; sea salt; Vegeta; pinch nutmeg; black pepper; extra-virgin olive oil; 1 tbsp sour-cream butter

**Method**
1. Clean and dry the swordfish; mix olive oil, lemon juice, chopped rosemary, salt and pepper in a bag, add the fish, coat well and marinate in the refrigerator **1 hour**.
2. (Alongside: slow-roast the aubergine with the garlic 50 minutes at 150 °C; blanch and pan-sear the artichokes; wilt the greens in butter; toast the pine nuts.)
3. Remove the fish from the marinade, strip the herbs, keep the oil; vacuum-seal and cook in the water bath at **59 °C for 20 minutes**.
4. Hold the bag in cold water **2 minutes** to stop the cook.
5. Cut the fish into 2 cm cubes; sear in a preheated cast-iron pan with 2 tbsp olive oil, **40 seconds per side**, until crispy — do not drag the pan.
6. **Pull at 59 °C (138 °F)** — the source's bath temperature; the sear adds surface only. Plate with the vegetables, pine nuts and herbs. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.

All five sources publish temperatures — full observed pull range **49–59 °C**, with three of
five clustered at **54.4–55.5 °C**. Mapped to `cooking_data.py`: the cluster sits squarely in

55.5 °C as the firmer alternative). The spread is driven by the two ways sous vide is sold:
technique sources pick one "perfect" medium number and stay there; home-cook sources pick
texture adjectives ("lightly flaky and firm" vs. firm-and-flaky) and back into a number.
`recommended_doneness` for the method: **`medium` (54–56 °C)** — the three-source cluster —
with the 49 °C rare-leaning and 59 °C firm-leaning traditions noted as legitimate houses
outside it, and the cut-level `well_done` default unchanged.

## What makes this method special

A sealed water bath removes both gradients that every other method here manages: after 20–45
minutes the entire steak is at the bath temperature to within a fraction of a degree, so the
edge and the centre are identical — the one doneness geometry no pan, grill or oven can
produce. For swordfish this matters unusually much, because the cut's failure mode is
textural: above ~60 °C the dense muscle segments expel moisture and turn chalky, and a bath
simply never gets there unless you set it there. The long hold also does quiet work the dry
methods can't — the moderate intramuscular fat softens gently, and the bag keeps every
volatile aromatic (lemon, herbs, butter) in contact with the flesh instead of driving it off
as steam. What it cannot do is brown: Maillard flavour must be added afterwards with a
1–2 minute sear (four of five sources do), which is why the German source's two-phase
"sous vide then screaming cast iron" approach exists.

## Summary

Sous vide cooks a swordfish steak to one exact temperature edge-to-edge — usually 54–56 °C
for about half an hour — then most cooks sear it briefly for a crust. It is an excellent
match for this cut: swordfish's dense flesh survives the long wet hold without falling apart,
and the method removes the overcooking risk that plagues every hot, fast technique for it.
The trade is that the fish comes out of the bag uniform and ungilded, so the sear afterwards
isn't optional if you want browning. Choose it when precision and juiciness matter more than
smoke or char.
