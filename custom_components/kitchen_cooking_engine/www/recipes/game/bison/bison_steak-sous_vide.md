# Bison Steak × Sous Vide — Recipe Temperature Research

## Cut profile

Sous vide is the method that solves bison's single biggest problem — the narrow window between
perfect and ruined — by removing the window entirely, and it is the only method for this cut
where every fetched source publishes its bath setting. The four sources span **120–132 °F
(49–55 °C)** for 1.5–4 hours plus one deliberately exotic two-stage low-temperature "warm
aging" cook (1 h at 39.5 °C then 1 h at 49.5 °C, aiming at the meat's own enzymes rather than
at pasteurisation). The US blog cluster wants the meat rare: the Great Range-sponsored post
holds the bath at **120 °F** and lifts it to **128 °F** only for the last hour ("the secret to
perfect bison was 120 °F"), the homestead blog from Maine holds **131 °F** for medium-rare,
and the Texas ranch guide offers 130 °F with a stated house preference of 132 °F. All four
finish the same way: pat dry, then a hard sear of 60–120 seconds per side in a very hot pan,
grill pan or with a torch. The consensus underneath the spread is unanimous and is really the
same rule the whole cut obeys: **bison above ~135–140 °F / 57–60 °C is a mistake** — one
source puts "Please, do not do this with Bison!" on its medium-well row, another calls well
done "DEFINITELY NOT RECOMMENDED" for lean pastured animals.

## Source recipes

### 1. Primal Bison (MemphisRanch.com) — How to Sous Vide a Bison Steak (Greg Nott, United States)
**Inspired by**: https://primalbison.com/blogs/how-tos/how-to-sous-vide-a-bison-steak (fetched 2026-10-26). Primal Bison (US bison purveyor blog, associated with MemphisRanch.com), by Greg Nott, published 2023-10-27. US precision-cooking tradition.
**Serves**: not stated · **Prep**: 10 min · **Cook**: 2 h bath (2–3 h+ for thicker) · **Rest**: a few minutes after the sear

**Ingredients**
- Bison steak (grass-fed, free-range; the source gives no piece count or weight)
- Sea salt; freshly ground black pepper; other herbs or spices as preferred
- High-smoke-point oil for the sear (the source likes light olive or avocado oil)

**Method**
1. Season the steak and vacuum-seal it (the source's frozen shortcut: drop the original vacuum packaging straight into the water and add an hour to the cook).
2. Set the bath: **130 °F (54 °C) for medium-rare, 140 °F (60 °C) for medium; "our preferred temperature is 132 °F (55.5 °C)"**.
3. Submerge the bag and cook **at least 2 hours** (2–3 h or more for thick steaks; the source has cooked steaks overnight: "they are even more tender and delicious").
4. Remove, pat dry with paper towel, and sear in a hot skillet or grill 60–90 seconds per side until a brown crust forms (a blowtorch is offered as the more even option).
5. Rest a few minutes; slice against the grain; serve.
6. **Bath setting stated per doneness: 130/132/140 °F (54/55.5/60 °C)**. The source's own steak chart (its oddity preserved verbatim): Rare 120–125 °F; Medium Rare 120–125 °F; Medium 130–135 °F; Medium Well 150–155 °F — "Please, do not do this with Bison!"; Well Done 160 °F+ — "Don't even think about it." Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source** (bath = core at these holds; seared after).

### 2. The Starving Chef — Mastering Sous Vide Bison Ribeye with Homemade Southwest Aioli (United States)
**Inspired by**: https://thestarvingchefblog.com/sous-vide-bison-ribeye-with-southwest-aioli/ (fetched 2026-10-26). The Starving Chef (US food blog); the post is **sponsored content by Great Range Brand Bison** (the source says so) and the recipe is credited to the blog's own test kitchen. US home-precision-cooking tradition. **Sponsored flag**: recorded as a brand-sponsored blog recipe, not as independent testing.
**Serves**: 2 · **Prep**: 30 min · **Cook**: 1–4 h bath · **Rest**: 10 min before searing + 5 min after

**Ingredients**
- 10 oz (285 g) bison ribeye (Great Range Brand Bison)
- 2 tsp salt; 2 tsp pepper
- **Southwest quick "aioli"**: ¼ cup mayonnaise; juice of 1 lime; 2 tbsp garlic powder; 1 tbsp chili powder; 1 tbsp cumin; 1 tbsp paprika; 1 tbsp coriander; 1 tsp cayenne; ½ tsp red pepper flakes; ½ tsp sea salt

**Method**
1. Whisk the aioli ingredients together and chill.
2. Bring the water to **120 °F** (the source's prose adds: the ideal bison temperature is "about 115–129 °F", up to 135 °F for those who don't like rare, and a Cleveland butcher's advice is quoted — "the secret to perfect bison was 120 °F").
3. Season the ribeye, seal it in a bag with no air (the water-displacement method and a straw-seal trick are described for those without a vacuum sealer).
4. Submerge and cook **1–4 hours**; during the **last hour raise the bath to 128 °F**.
5. Remove, rest **10 minutes**; preheat a greased grill pan until smoking; sear **1–2 minutes per side**, rotating 90° after about 90 seconds for grill lines; rest 5 more minutes.
6. Serve with the aioli and mashed potatoes. **Bath settings stated: 120 °F → 128 °F (49 → 53 °C)**; the source's stated ideal band is 115–129 °F. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.

### 3. Stefan's Gourmet — Bison Ribeye with Eggplant (Netherlands)
**Inspired by**: https://stefangourmet.com/2014/02/04/bison-ribeye-sous-vide-with-eggplant/ (fetched 2026-10-26). Stefan's Gourmet (Dutch food blog), published 2014-02-04. Dutch home-precision-cooking tradition. **Technique flag**: the cook is the author's self-named "warm aging" — holding the meat at enzyme-active temperatures rather than at pasteurisation temperature — recorded as the two-stage low-temperature technique it is.
**Serves**: 1 · **Prep**: 15 min · **Cook**: 2 h bath (two stages) · **Rest**: 5–10 min foil rest after the sear

**Ingredients**
- Bison ribeye steak
- Salt; freshly ground black pepper; clarified butter (or rendered bison/beef fat) for searing
- **Sauce**: red wine; bison or beef stock
- **Side**: eggplant (pink eggplant preferred), olive oil, salt, pepper, minced parsley

**Method**
1. Season the ribeye with salt and pepper; vacuum seal.
2. **Cook 1 hour at 39.5 °C (103 °F), then raise to 49.5 °C (121 °F) and cook 1 hour more.** (No-cooker variant: 40 °C water with the bag in it, covered; after an hour add boiling water to reach 50 °C.) The author's rationale: between ~40 and 50 °C the calpain and cathepsin enzymes age the meat far faster than fridge aging — "warm aging".
3. Meanwhile roast the eggplant slices at 225 °C (salted 30–45 min first, wiped, oiled) 15 + 10–15 min.
4. Pat the steak dry ("a dry steak is important for good browning"); sear in very hot clarified butter **about 2 minutes on the first side until nicely browned, 1–2 minutes on the second**.
5. Wrap in foil and rest **5–10 minutes** — the source states this brings the centre to **about 55 °C (131 °F), i.e. medium rare**.
6. Deglaze the pan with red wine, add stock, reduce to a syrupy sauce; fold in the steak's juices. Serve with the eggplant.
7. **Bath settings stated: 39.5 °C then 49.5 °C (103 → 121 °F)**; stated final centre after the rest: **55 °C / 131 °F (medium rare)**. Final serving temperature: **~55 °C / 131 °F** (stated).

### 4. Of Goats and Greens — Sous Vide Boneless Bison New York Sirloin Steak (United States)
**Inspired by**: https://goatsandgreens.wordpress.com/2020/05/19/recipe-boneless-bison-sirloin-steak-sous-vide-dinner/ (fetched 2026-10-26). Of Goats and Greens (US homestead food blog, western Massachusetts; the steak is named as from Hatmatack Buffalo Farm, Berwick, Maine), published 2020-05-19 (cooked 2020-04-07). US homestead tradition.
**Serves**: 2 · **Prep**: 5 min for the meat, 15 for the rest · **Cook**: 1.5–2 h bath · **Rest**: "with sous vide, resting is not needed"

**Ingredients**
- 10 oz (300 g) at least 1" thick bison steak (New York sirloin here; fully pastured beef works too)
- Scant ½ tsp ground ancho chili (or other mild chili; omittable)
- Salt; ground pepper
- **Pan finish**: 1 tbsp cooking oil; 1 baby bok choy; 1 leek (white + pale green); 1 bell pepper; ½ heaped tsp dried thyme; 2 heaped tsp aioli-garlic-mustard sauce (or 1 heaped tsp Dijon)

**Method**
1. Coat the steak lightly on both sides with salt, pepper and the ancho. Bag it (silicon bag with water displacement, or vacuum).
2. Set the bath to **131 °F (55 °C)** for medium rare; submerge the bagged steak and hold **1.5–2 hours** (the source's note: ¾" steaks or thinner don't sous vide well — the sear cooks them too far; supermarket steaks need only about an hour, range-finished meat tolerates 2 h+).
3. The source's bath chart (adapted by it from Serious Eats): very rare–rare 120–129 °F (49–53 °C) 1–2.5 h; medium-rare 130–134 °F (54–57 °C) 1–4 h; medium 135–144 °F (57–62 °C) 1–4 h; medium-well 145–155 °F (63–68 °C) 1–3.5 h ("not recommended" for lean pastured animals); well done 156 °F+ (69 °C+) "DEFINITELY NOT RECOMMENDED WITH PASTURED / GRASS FINISHED ANIMALS".
4. Sauté the vegetables (bok choy, leek, pepper, thyme) in the oil; when nearly done, unbag the steak, pat dry, raise the pan to medium-high and sear **about a minute per side plus the long edges**; add the bag juices to the skillet and the mustard to the meat, let the juices and mustard meld into the vegetables.
5. Plate the meat with the vegetables and spoon the sauce over. **Bath setting stated: 131 °F (55 °C)** for medium rare. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source** (its own chart: no rest needed with sous vide).

## Temperature consensus

| Source | Tradition | Bath setting(s) | Time | Finish |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primal Bison (US) | US purveyor precision guide | **130 °F MR / 132 °F house pick / 140 °F medium** (54–60 °C) | ≥2 h (overnight fine) | 60–90 s/side sear or torch |
| The Starving Chef (US, Great Range-sponsored) | US home precision cooking | **120 °F → 128 °F** (49 → 53 °C); ideal band 115–129 °F | 1–4 h | 10 min rest, grill-pan sear 1–2 min/side |
| Stefan's Gourmet (NL) | Dutch "warm aging" two-stage | **39.5 °C → 49.5 °C** (103 → 121 °F) | 1 h + 1 h | hot clarified-butter sear; foil rest → **~55 °C centre** |
| Of Goats and Greens (US) | US homestead precision cooking | **131 °F (55 °C)** MR chart 120–155 °F | 1.5–2 h (1–4 h band) | ~1 min/side pan sear; no rest needed |

All four sources publish numbers, and the corpus defines a bath band of **120–132 °F (49–55 °C)**
for the rare-to-medium-rare school with holds of 1–4 hours, plus one enzyme-focused two-stage
cook below pasteurisation temperature. The spread is not random: the two blog cooks who buy
into the "bison is best rare" argument hold 120–128 °F, the two who aim at a finished medium-
rare steak hold 130–132 °F / 131 °F, and the warm-ager spends his two hours below 122 °F and
lets the sear and a 5–10 minute rest walk the centre up to a stated 55 °C. The one thing every
source agrees on is the ceiling — Primal Bison's chart puts a joke on the medium-well row and
a second joke on well done, the Maine homestead blog calls well done "DEFINITELY NOT
RECOMMENDED" for pastured animals, and the sponsored blog says "any higher than that and
you'll risk overcooking". **Tie-break**: four measured sources, three of them holding 120–131 °F
for a rare-to-medium-rare result; the median of the house settings (128, 130, 131, 132) is
**130–131 °F (54–55 °C)**. **Applied target**: **55 °C (131 °F) for 1.5–4 h** — the middle of
the measured band, the setting the two medium-rare sources agree on, with 120–128 °F recorded
as the rare school's deliberate alternative. **Temperature used in the cut file: 55 °C bath
(bath = core at these holds), inside the existing `medium_rare` band**. `recommended_doneness`
for this method: **`medium_rare`** — the whole measured band, including its low end, is aimed
at rare-to-medium-rare, and every source forbids medium-well and beyond.

## What makes this method special

For a meat whose entire culinary identity is "do not overcook", the water bath is the obvious
instrument and this corpus treats it that way: bison is the cut where the sous-vide pitch is
not restaurant showmanship but risk removal. A lean steak has no intramuscular fat to mask a
two-degree overshoot, and on a hot grill or pan the margin is seconds; in a bath at 130 °F the
margin is hours, which is why the ranch source cheerfully cooks steaks overnight and the
Maine blog notes range-finished meat only gets better at 2+ hours. The finishing sear is the
one step all four sources insist on — the bag cannot crust, and for bison the crust matters
even more than for beef because it is the only Maillard the lean surface will get. The
divergence worth recording is the rare-versus-medium-rare argument inside the US sources: the
120 °F school (a Cleveland butcher's "secret", quoted twice across two blogs) versus the
130–132 °F school, with the Dutch warm-ager splitting the difference by cooking low and
letting the rest finish the walk to 55 °C. The thickness rule from the Maine source is the
practical footnote: sous vide wants steaks of at least an inch — thinner cuts lose their
advantage to the sear.

## Summary

Sous vide is bison's risk-removal tool: four sources, four published settings, one band — 120–132 °F (49–55 °C) for 1–4 hours, always followed by a hard one-minute finishing sear. The US sources split between a deliberate rare (120–128 °F) and a finished medium-rare (130–132 °F), the Dutch warm-ager cooks at 103→121 °F and rests to a stated 55 °C, and all four put an explicit ceiling at medium. The practical middle is 131 °F (55 °C) for 1.5–4 h with a dry, very hot sear and no long rest; use steaks at least an inch thick. Every source that names a maximum says the same thing in a different tone — from "Please, do not do this with Bison!" to "DEFINITELY NOT RECOMMENDED".
