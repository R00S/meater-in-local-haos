# Buffalo Burger × Pan Fry — Recipe Temperature Research

## Cut profile

A pan gives a ground bison patty the one thing a grill cannot: continuous contact with a hot
surface on one whole face, and its own rendered fat (or the cook's added oil) to fry in. The
cut is coarse-ground bison from the shoulder, round or a trim blend, typically 90/10 to 93/7
lean-to-fat, formed into a patty of 110–180 g and roughly 2 cm thick. That leanness is what
makes the pan the most controllable method and also the least forgiving: with almost no
internal fat there is no basting reservoir, so the pan has to supply the oil deliberately, and
the same contact that builds the crust will squeeze the patty dry if the cook presses it. All
three sources here treat the pan as a heat-management tool — one works at medium-high with a
gentle press-free sear, one uses the pan for a deliberate smash, and the Swedish producer
turns the heat **down to half** after the fat is hot, which is the opposite instinct to the US
rows and the most useful disagreement in the leaf. Carryover is a couple of degrees over a
short rest: the patty is thin and the pan is hot, so the gradient inside is steep and
short-lived.

## Source recipes

### 1. Amanda's Cookin' (Chef Antoine Davis, US) — Buffalo Burger (skillet)
**Inspired by**: https://amandascookin.com/buffalo-burger/ (fetched 2026-10-26). Amanda's Cookin' (US home-cooking site; recipe credited to Chef Antoine Davis, "Cuisine: American"). US skillet tradition — **Culinary Group B** source.
**Serves**: 3 · **Prep**: 15 min · **Cook**: 10 min · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 1 tbsp olive oil, plus 1 tsp more if the pan is dry
- ½ cup minced red onion
- 1 jalapeño pepper, minced, seeds removed
- 450 g (1 lb) ground bison
- 1 tsp chili powder
- 2 tsp garlic salt
- 3 burger buns

**Method**
1. Heat the olive oil in a skillet over medium-high until shimmering. Add the red onion and jalapeño and sauté until tender and browned; remove them, leaving as much oil in the pan as possible.
2. Put the ground bison in a bowl, press it up the sides, and sprinkle with the chili powder and garlic salt. Add the cooked onion and jalapeño and combine with gloved hands until evenly distributed.
3. Divide into **3 equal patties** and press a thumb indent into the centre of each.
4. If the pan is dry, add another teaspoon of oil. Cook over **medium-high heat, about 5 minutes per side, flipping a couple of times** through the cook.
5. **Do not press the patties.** The source's own reason: bison is so lean that pressing "squeezes all the juices out — this isn't a smash burger folks!".
6. Probe horizontally from the edge into the centre of the thickest patty.
7. **Pull at 71 °C (160 °F)** — the source's stated figure ("ground bison should be cooked to an internal temperature of 160F registered on an instant read thermometer inserted into the thickest portion"), while noting that "you can cook bison to medium-rare or medium (just as you would a beef burger)". Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.
8. Add cheese if using, and build on a bun with lettuce, tomato, red onion and pickles.

### 2. Jackson Hole Buffalo Meat (Sarah Knight, US) — Ground Bison Smash Burger
**Inspired by**: https://jhbuffalomeat.com/blogs/news/bison-smash-burger-1 (fetched 2026-10-26). Jackson Hole Buffalo Meat recipe blog, "Cuisine: American". US smash-burger tradition — **Culinary Group B** source. **Technique flag**: this is the pan's opposite school — the patty is deliberately flattened into the metal, which the other two sources forbid for lean bison. It is filed here because it is the only fetched record of the smash variant on bison, and because it shows how the lean problem is solved by speed rather than by gentleness: 4–5 oz pressed thin and cooked in about a minute.
**Serves**: 2–4 · **Prep**: 5 min (+20 min salting) · **Cook**: 5–10 min · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 4 bison burger patties (the source uses the producer's pre-formed patties, ~4–5 oz / 115–140 g each)
- 4 slices American cheese
- Fried or pickled onions; ketchup
- 2–4 buns
- Lard, or oil of choice, for the pan

**Method**
1. Take the patties out, salt both sides, and let them sit about **20 minutes**.
2. Immediately before cooking, roll each patty into a **loose ball** — the source calls this the trick of the smash burger, because a ball pressed flat in the pan gives an uneven, lacy edge that crisps.
3. Warm an **8-inch frying pan** over **medium-high** with lard or oil. (The source notes a flat-top griddle works too.)
4. Place a ball in the centre of the hot pan and **press it down hard with a spatula**.
5. Sear until the brown from the bottom starts to creep through to the top, then **flip**, add the cheese, and cook **about 30 seconds** more.
6. Probe at the edge, through the thin part of the patty, angled into the centre — a smashed patty is only a few millimetres thick in places, so the probe must sit in the middle of the thickest fold.
7. **Pull at 71 °C (160 °F)** — house guidance, **not the source's number**: the page gives no internal temperature and its endpoint is the visual cue plus the 30 seconds after the flip. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**; it goes straight from pan to bun with toppings.
8. Serve on the bun with onions and ketchup; the source's own advice is not to overload it — "let the flavor of the ground Buffalo meat shine through".

### 3. Wrågården i Falköping (Sweden) — Bisonburgare (pan-fry section)
**Inspired by**: https://wragarden.se/vara-produkter-du-hittar-vara-produkter-hos/bisonburgare/ (fetched 2026-10-26). Wrågården, Swedish bison farm in Falköping, cooking instructions for its own bison-burger product. Swedish farm-producer tradition — **Culinary Group D** source. **Multi-method flag**: the page's grill section is recorded in the `grill` leaf; this record covers its **stekpanna** (frying-pan) section. **Composition flag**: 60 % Swedish bison, 40 % Swedish pork, salt, pepper.
**Serves**: not stated · **Prep**: not stated · **Cook**: 5–9 min · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- Bisonburgare from Wrågården: **60 % Swedish bison, 40 % Swedish pork**, salt, pepper
- Cooking fat for the pan (*matfett* — the source names no type)

**Method**
1. Heat the fat in the pan and wait until it **has taken on colour and stopped bubbling** — the source's doneness cue for the fat itself.
2. Lay the burgers in the pan, then **lower the heat to half effect** (*sänk värmen till halv effekt*).
3. Fry **about 3 minutes on one side** before the first turn, then turn a few more times until the burgers have had **about 9 minutes total from frozen, or 5–6 minutes if thawed**.
4. Probe from the side into the centre of the thickest burger.
5. **Pull at 70 °C (158 °F)** — **the source's own published floor**: *"Innertemperaturen skall vara minst 70 °C"*. It is one degree below the 71 °C the US sources quote, and it belongs to a patty that is 40 % pork.
6. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**; it gives no rest.
7. Serve with oven-roasted root vegetables, freshly baked bread, coleslaw and smoky dressing.

## Temperature consensus

| Source | Tradition | Published doneness evidence | Internal temperature |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amanda's Cookin' (US) | US skillet, medium-high, 5 min/side, no pressing | instant-read in thickest part; "medium-rare or medium (just as you would a beef burger)" allowed | **160 °F (71 °C)** |
| Bison Smash Burger (US) | US smash on medium-high, pressed, ~30 s after flip | visual — brown creeping through — plus 30 s | Not stated |
| Wrågården (Sweden) | Swedish farm producer, fat hot then **half heat**, 5–6 min thawed | time only, plus a hard floor | **at least 70 °C (158 °F)** |

Two numbers, one degree apart, from two different countries and two different products: the
US skillet recipe's **160 °F** and the Swedish producer's **at least 70 °C**. The smash record
contributes no number, which is honest for a technique whose entire endpoint is a visual
change in under two minutes. Note the shape of the disagreement here — unlike the grill leaf,
both pan sources are *imperative* rather than permissive: Amanda's Cookin' names 160 °F first
and only then allows medium-rare, and Wrågården states its 70 °C as a floor that must be met.
Mapped to the engine, 70–71 °C is exactly `DONENESS_WELL_DONE` (68–77 °C, target 71), so
`recommended_doneness` for this method is **`well_done`**, and no override is needed.

## What makes this method special

The pan is the only one of this cut's methods where the cook controls the fat. On a grill the
fat drips away; in a smoker the surface never gets hot enough to render properly; in an air
fryer the basket drains by design. In a pan, the oil that goes in is what the crust is built
from, and the two US sources are explicit that bison needs it added — Amanda's Cookin' tops up
the pan rather than let it go dry, and the smash recipe reaches for lard. The second
distinguishing mechanic is contact pressure, and this leaf is where the corpus genuinely
disagrees with itself: two sources say never press because lean bison will give up its juice,
and the third's whole technique is a hard press in the first thirty seconds. The reconciliation
is timing, not principle — the smash press happens once, at the start, when the surface is
still cold and the interior has not yet heated, while the press the other two forbid is a
repeated mid-cook squeeze that pushes out juice the patty has already started to hold. The
Swedish row adds the third idea: turn the heat *down* once the fat is hot. It is the opposite
of the US instinct to sear at medium-high throughout, and it is what a 40 %-pork patty can
afford — a pure lean bison patty cooked at half heat would steam rather than brown, which is
the honest limit of that row's transferability.

## Summary

Pan-frying a bison burger means supplying the fat yourself and then leaving the patty alone:
medium-high heat, 5 minutes a side, one or two turns, no mid-cook pressing. The exception is
the smash — a loose ball pressed hard once, at the start, flipped after the brown creeps
through and finished in about thirty seconds, which turns the leanness into crisp edges rather
than dryness. The Swedish producer's half-heat method belongs to a bison-and-pork product and
does not transfer to a lean patty. Pull at 70–71 °C and rest briefly; there is nothing in the
fat to keep cooking it for you.
