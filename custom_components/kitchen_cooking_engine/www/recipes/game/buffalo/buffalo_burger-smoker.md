# Buffalo Burger × Smoker — Recipe Temperature Research

## Cut profile

A smoker is the odd method out for this cut: it cooks a ground patty at a temperature below the
point where a crust can form, so the patty is not seared at all until — in one of these two
records — it is taken off and seared on something else. The cut is the same lean coarse-ground
bison, formed into patties here **thicker than any other method in this corpus**: 6 oz (170 g)
rounded patties in the US record and eight roughly 115 g patties in the Canadian one, both
built specifically to survive a long cook. Thickness is the point. A lean bison patty has no
fat cap to insulate it, so the smoker's low, even, humid air is the only way to bring the
centre up slowly without the outside drying to leather first — and both sources bulk up the
mixture with fat and moisture for the same reason, one with bacon and cheddar stuffed inside,
the other with breadcrumbs, egg and onion. Carryover is meaningfully different from the other
methods here: the cook is an hour at 107–120 °C, the patty is thick, and it comes off the heat
as a whole mass, so the rest is where the last degrees arrive.

## Source recipes

### 1. With the Woodruffs (Mason Woodruff, US) — Chipotle Cheddar Smoked Bison Burgers
**Inspired by**: https://withthewoodruffs.com/traeger-smoked-bison-burgers/ (fetched 2026-10-26). With the Woodruffs (US recipe blog), tested on a Traeger Pro 575 with Pro Blend pellets. US low-and-slow pellet-smoker tradition — **Culinary Group B** source.
**Serves**: 7 · **Prep**: 30 min · **Cook**: 1 h 30 min · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 900 g (2 lb) ground bison, **90/10**
- 4 slices bacon
- 1 large sweet onion, finely diced
- 4–5 chipotle peppers in adobo (*chipotles en adobo* — smoked jalapeños in a tomato-vinegar sauce), minced
- ½ tbsp kosher salt; ½ tsp black pepper
- 115 g (4 oz) shredded cheddar cheese
- 7 slices sharp cheddar; 7 brioche burger buns
- Guacamole and pickled onions, to serve

**Method**
1. Cook the bacon from a cold skillet over medium heat until fully done; drain on paper towel, then cook the onion in the same pan with the salt and pepper for 6–8 minutes until soft and picking up colour. Stir in the chipotles and cook until fragrant, 60–90 seconds. Transfer to a dish and put it in the freezer to cool while the smoker preheats.
2. Preheat the smoker to **225 °F (107 °C)**, lid closed.
3. Once the onion mixture is at least at room temperature, crumble the bacon into it with the shredded cheddar. Pat the ground bison dry with paper towels, then break it into the mixture and combine evenly by hand.
4. Divide into **seven 6-ounce (170 g) patties with rounded edges**, handling as little as possible, and make them slightly wider than the buns. Season lightly with salt and pepper.
5. Probe one patty from the side, tip at the geometric centre — these are thick stuffed patties, so keep the tip out of the cheese pocket.
6. Smoke at **225 °F for about 1 hour, until the centres reach 140 °F (60 °C)**.
7. **Once 140 °F is reached, hold a further 12 minutes** before adding cheese — the source's food-safety argument, quoted: *"The USDA recommends cooking ground meats to an internal temperature of 160 °F, but you can pasteurize ground meats by holding at a temperature of 140 °F for at least 12 minutes"* (attributed to ThermoWorks via the Hospitality Institute of Technology and Management, and Serious Eats). It adds that going rarer would require holding at 130 °F for two hours, and that a very rare burger needs freshly ground meat.
8. Add a slice of cheese to each and cook **5 minutes more** with the lid closed to melt.
9. **Pull at 60 °C (140 °F) plus a 12-minute hold** — the source's stated pasteurisation equivalent, not a conventional instant-read target. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.
10. Serve on toasted buns with guacamole and pickled onions. The source also describes a reverse-sear finish (sear both sides in a hot skillet or on a grill at maximum heat after the hold) and a smash-burger application of the same topping mixture.

### 2. Dish 'n' the Kitchen (Canada) — Backyard Smoked Bison Burgers
**Inspired by**: https://dishnthekitchen.com/backyard-smoked-bison-burgers/ (fetched 2026-10-26, published 2018-06-07). Dish 'n' the Kitchen (Canadian food blog; the bison comes from the author's uncle's farm). Canadian charcoal-smoker tradition — **Culinary Group B** source (Canada, distinct from the US rows).
**Serves**: 6 · **Prep**: 20 min · **Cook**: 1 h · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 900 g (2 lb) ground bison
- ⅓ cup breadcrumbs
- 1 egg, beaten
- Salt and pepper
- ⅓ cup onion, finely chopped
- 1 tsp chili powder
- 1 tbsp garlic powder
- 1 tsp Worcestershire sauce
- ½ large Vidalia onion (sweet onion) + a few pats of butter, for smoking in foil
- Medium Gouda, tomato, lettuce and rhubarb barbecue sauce, to serve

**Method**
1. **Onions**: slice the half onion into rings, season with salt and pepper, dot with butter, wrap loosely in foil and cook over medium heat for about an hour.
2. **Burgers**: put the ground bison and seasonings in a large bowl, add the egg and breadcrumbs, mix thoroughly, divide into **8 equal portions**, flatten into burgers and refrigerate until the smoker is ready.
3. Soak wood chips in water. Light about half the charcoal in a chimney with paper; it takes about 20 minutes to become red hot. Load the rest of the charcoal so the smoker will run **1.5–2 hours**, then mix the hot charcoal through it.
4. When the smoker is up to temperature (**medium-high**, the source's only setting description), add the drained wood chips to the coals, fill the water pan and set it on the lower rack.
5. Probe a burger from the side into the centre — the patties are thin enough that an instant-read check at the end is the practical route.
6. Cook the burgers **45–60 minutes**, depending on how hot the smoker runs.
7. **Pull at 65 °C (150 °F)** — **the source's own target**: "to target internal temperature of 65C (150F) check with meat thermometer".
8. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**. Optional: put a slice of cheese on each burger 5–10 minutes before the end so it melts.

## Temperature consensus

| Source | Tradition | Published doneness evidence | Internal temperature |
|---|---|---|---|
| With the Woodruffs (US) | US pellet smoker, 225 °F, 1 h + 12 min hold | core temperature plus a pasteurisation hold; USDA 160 °F quoted | **140 °F (60 °C) held 12 min** |
| Dish 'n' the Kitchen (Canada) | Canadian charcoal smoker + water pan, 45–60 min | meat thermometer at the end | **150 °F (65 °C)** |

Two numbers and two completely different safety arguments, which is what makes this leaf worth
having. The Canadian source cooks to an ordinary instant-read endpoint — **65 °C / 150 °F**,
five degrees below the cut's USDA floor and six below the engine's `well_done` target. The US
source cooks to **60 °C** but does not treat that as the endpoint at all: it holds the patty at
that temperature for twelve minutes, which is time-at-temperature pasteurisation rather than an
instant-read threshold, and it cites the equivalence explicitly instead of pretending 140 °F is
simply "safe". Mapping to the engine: neither number is a plain `well_done` reading, and the
cut's registry carries only `DONENESS_WELL_DONE` (68–77 °C, target 71). The honest application
is therefore to keep the engine's 71 °C target for an ordinary cook and to record here that the
two published protocols reach a safe result **below** it by holding longer — 65 °C at the end
of a 45–60 minute cook, or 60 °C plus twelve minutes. `recommended_doneness` for this method:
**`well_done`**, with the hold protocol recorded as a note, not as a new band; a per-method
range that dips to 60 °C would require a `medium`-style band that this cut does not carry and
that a ground patty should not advertise by default.

## What makes this method special

The smoker is the only method here that can overcook a bison patty in the direction of *dryness
without burning* — there is no surface hot enough to char, so the failure is invisible until the
patty is cut. That is why both sources build the patty up before it goes in: the US record
stuffs bacon, cheddar and chipotle through the middle, which is fat and moisture placed where
the heat has to travel furthest, and the Canadian one adds egg, breadcrumbs and chopped onion,
which is the same insurance in a different form. The second distinguishing mechanic is that
smoke is a surface treatment applied over time rather than a crust: both sources run a water pan
or a stuffed interior to keep the surface from drying while the smoke deposits, and the US
recipe explicitly offers a **reverse sear** afterwards for cooks who want the crust the smoker
cannot give — which is an admission that this method produces flavour and evenness, not
browning. The third is the safety argument, and it is the most valuable thing in the leaf: a
low, long cook is the one context where time-at-temperature pasteurisation makes sense for a
ground patty, because the cook already lasts an hour. It is also the one context where a
thermometer is not optional — at 225 °F a bison patty goes from 140 °F to 160 °F faster than
the cook expects, and there is no crust colour to read.

## Summary

Smoking a bison burger means 45–60 minutes at roughly 225 °F on a thick, built-up patty — bacon
and cheddar stuffed inside, or egg and breadcrumbs bound through — because a lean patty with
nothing to lose will dry out before it browns, and a smoker never browns it anyway. The two
sources disagree usefully on the endpoint: 150 °F on a thermometer, or 140 °F held twelve
minutes as a pasteurisation equivalent. Take the reverse-sear option if you want a crust, keep
the water pan full, and do not walk away from the last twenty degrees.
