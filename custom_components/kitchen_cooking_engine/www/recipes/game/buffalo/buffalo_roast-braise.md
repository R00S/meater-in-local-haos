# Buffalo Roast × Braise — Recipe Temperature Research

## Cut profile

Braising is the method that turns this cut's weakness into its virtue. The same dense,
fat-starved fibre bundles that dry out in an oven become fork-tender in a covered pot, because
moist heat at 80–150 °C lets the collagen in the chuck's connective tissue gelatinise instead of
contracting. The cut is a **chuck/shoulder or inside-round roast** of 1–2 kg, bone-in or boneless,
and the structure that matters is not marbling but the **collagen sheath** around each muscle
bundle: it needs hours at temperature, not a fast sear. Every source here therefore uses the same
three-part sequence — brown the surface, deglaze with wine or stock, then cover and leave it — and
none of them measures a final internal temperature, because the doneness signal is mechanical
(the meat gives way under a fork). Carryover is negligible by comparison with oven roasting: the
pot is a sealed thermal mass, the liquid buffers swings, and the roast comes out at a temperature
it has already held for an hour.

## Source recipes

### 1. Jackson Hole Buffalo Meat (Pamela Sinclair, US) — Simple Braised Buffalo Pot Roast
**Inspired by**: https://jhbuffalomeat.com/blogs/news/simple-braised-buffalo-pot-roast (fetched 2026-10-26, published 2018-08-02). Credited by the page to *A Taste of Wyoming Cookbook* by Pamela Sinclair; "Category: Buffalo". Wyoming/US ranch-country tradition — **Culinary Group B** source.
**Serves**: 4 · **Prep**: 20 min · **Cook**: 2½ h · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 2 tbsp olive oil
- 1.4 kg (3 lb) buffalo (bison) chuck roast — the page's alternative is elk chuck roast
- 1 onion, coarsely chopped
- 4 large carrots, peeled and chopped
- 2 stalks celery, diced
- 5 medium potatoes, quartered
- 1 bay leaf
- 2 tbsp tomato paste
- 1 cup dry red wine
- 1 cup stock
- 2 tbsp flour
- Salt and pepper to taste

**Method**
1. Preheat the oven to **300 °F (149 °C)**.
2. Heat the oil in a Dutch oven over medium-high and brown the roast evenly on all sides. Add the onion, carrots, celery, bay leaf, salt and pepper and sauté to lightly brown, about 1 minute.
3. Stir in the tomato paste, red wine and stock and cook over medium heat for 1 minute, stirring the vegetables around the roast to deglaze the pan. Add the potatoes and stir to coat them in the liquid.
4. Cover and cook in the oven **2–2½ hours**.
5. There is **no probe step and no internal temperature** in this record — the source's doneness signal is the braise time plus the meat giving way under a fork.
6. Transfer the roast and vegetables to a platter with a slotted spoon.
7. Put the Dutch oven over a medium burner, add the flour to the drippings and whisk several minutes until thickened; serve as gravy.
8. **Pull at 93 °C (199 °F) internal, fork-tender** — house guidance, **not the source's number**: the page publishes none, and 93 °C is the house figure for collagen-gelatinised braised beef-family roasts. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.

### 2. Food Network (US) — Best Friend's Bison Pot Roast
**Inspired by**: https://www.foodnetwork.com/recipes/best-friends-bison-pot-roast-2269310 (fetched 2026-10-26). Food Network recipe page (bison inside round; "Cook: 45 min, Inactive: 8 hr"). US pot-roast tradition — **Culinary Group B** source. **Cut-form flag**: the roast here is an **inside round**, the leanest sub-cut of the roast form — not a chuck.
**Serves**: 4 · **Prep**: 10 min · **Cook**: 45 min active + 7–8 h covered · **Rest**: 10 min

**Ingredients**
- 1 × 900 g (2 lb) bison inside round roast, fresh or fully thawed
- ⅓ cup flour
- 2 tbsp chopped or granulated garlic
- 1 tsp coarse sea salt; 1 tsp coarse ground pepper
- ⅓ cup olive oil
- 1 large onion, diced; 3 medium carrots, diced; 2 stalks celery; 1 large leek, sliced
- 1 tbsp tomato paste
- 2½ cups beef stock, **or** 1½ cups beef stock plus 1 cup dry red wine

**Method**
1. Put the bison roast in a large zip-lock bag with the flour, garlic, salt and pepper. Shake to coat and rest **10 minutes**.
2. Preheat a heavy-bottomed pan on the stove over medium-low for 10 minutes; add the oil and brown the roast **7 minutes on the first side and 5 minutes on the second**, then set aside.
3. In the same pan, sauté the onion until soft and clear, about 9 minutes; add the carrots and celery and cook a further 11 minutes until the vegetables caramelise. Add the tomato paste and sauté 2 minutes more.
4. Add the stock (or stock and wine), return the roast to the pan, bring to a boil and immediately reduce to a simmer.
5. **Cover and choose one heat source**: the lowest stovetop setting for **7 hours**, **or** an oven at **180 °F (82 °C)** for **8 hours**, **or** a slow cooker on **low for 8 hours**.
6. When the meat is fork tender, remove the roast and rest **10 minutes** before slicing.
7. There is **no internal temperature** in this record; the source's signal is "fork tender" after the long covered cook.
8. **Pull at 93 °C (199 °F) internal, fork-tender** — house guidance, **not the source's number**. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.

### 3. Red Cliff Band of Lake Superior Chippewa / Sean Sherman (Indigenous North American) — Bison Pot Roast with Hominy
**Inspired by**: https://www.redclifffish.com/bison-pot-roast-with-hominy/ (fetched 2026-10-26). Published by Red Cliff as part of "Sean Sherman's 10 Essential Native American Recipes"; Sean Sherman is Oglala Lakota. Indigenous North American tradition — **Culinary Group B** source. **Same-author note**: this is the same recipe family as the "Cedar-Braised Bison" record that previously sat in this leaf from thekitchn.com (a different published adaptation, with maple syrup and sumac instead of agave and dandelion greens). That older record is **replaced** by this verified version rather than counted twice.
**Serves**: not stated (a 1.4–1.8 kg roast) · **Prep**: overnight hominy soak + 15 min · **Cook**: 3–3½ h · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 1 cup dried hominy, soaked overnight
- 1 × 1.4–1.8 kg (3–4 lb) bison chuck roast
- Coarse sea salt (2 tbsp for seasoning, plus more to finish)
- 3 tbsp sunflower oil
- 3–4 cups bison or vegetable stock
- 2 fresh sage sprigs, plus more leaves for garnish
- 1 × 4-inch branch wild white cedar, **or** 2 tsp dried juniper berries
- ¼ cup light agave nectar
- 1 packed cup sliced dandelion greens (or spinach or kale)

**Method**
1. Soak the hominy in a large bowl covered by 3 inches of water overnight at room temperature; drain and discard the soaking liquid.
2. Heat the oven to **250 °F (121 °C)**. Season the bison generously on all sides with 2 tbsp salt.
3. Heat the oil in a large Dutch oven over medium-high and sear the bison until browned on all sides, rotating it when it releases easily — **about 15 minutes total**. Transfer the meat to a plate.
4. Add the drained hominy, stock, sage, cedar (or juniper) and agave to the pot. Bring to a simmer over high heat, scraping the bottom, then return the meat to the pot.
5. Cover, transfer to the oven and bake until the meat is very tender, **3–3½ hours**, turning the bison about halfway through.
6. Discard the sage and cedar (juniper berries can stay — the source notes they add flavour and texture when eaten with the meat). Stir in the greens until wilted and season the liquid with salt.
7. There is **no internal temperature** in this record; the source's signal is "very tender" at 3–3½ hours.
8. **Pull at 93 °C (199 °F) internal, fork-tender** — house guidance, **not the source's number**. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.
9. Slice the meat into 1-inch slabs, spoon the sauce, hominy and greens over, and garnish with sage.

## Temperature consensus

| Source | Tradition | Vessel and oven | Time | Published internal temperature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| JH Buffalo / *A Taste of Wyoming* (US) | Wyoming ranch-country Dutch oven | **300 °F (149 °C)**, covered | 2–2½ h | None — fork-tender |
| Food Network (US) | US pot roast, inside round | **180 °F (82 °C)** oven, **or** slow cooker / stovetop | 7–8 h | None — "fork tender" |
| Red Cliff / Sean Sherman (Indigenous NA) | Lakota, hominy and cedar | **250 °F (121 °C)**, covered Dutch oven | 3–3½ h | None — "very tender" |

Not one of the three publishes an internal temperature, and that is the honest finding of this
leaf: braised bison is cooked to a **texture**, not to a number. The three oven settings spread
across **82–149 °C**, which is a much wider range than this cut's other methods show, and the
reason is time — the 82 °C oven is paired with 8 hours, the 149 °C oven with 2½. There is also a
physical limit worth recording: an oven set to 82 °C cannot take the interior above about 82 °C,
so the Food Network record's fork-tenderness at that setting comes from **eight hours at a
temperature where collagen still gelatinises slowly**, not from reaching the 90+ °C that the
other two reach inside their hotter ovens. Mapping to the engine: the cut's `medium_rare` band
(52–57 °C) is meaningless for this method — the meat passes through it in the first hour and
keeps going. `recommended_doneness` for this method is therefore recorded as the cut's default
label only because the engine has no braise-specific band, and the **house pull of 93 °C
(199 °F)** is what the probe should be used for; the registry's temperature ranges are not a
sensible target for a braise, which is a data limitation this leaf records rather than hides.

## What makes this method special

Three things separate braising from every other method in this cut's corpus. First, **the
temperature ceiling stops mattering**: a probe reading taken in a braise tells you how far along
the collagen conversion is, not whether the meat is "done", which is why all three sources use a
fork instead of a number. Second, **the liquid is the heat medium**: at 250–300 °F oven
temperature the meat is cooked by conduction from a liquid that cannot exceed 100 °C at
atmospheric pressure, so the interior is effectively clamped near the boiling point no matter how
hot the oven is — the 149 °C oven and the 121 °C oven differ in how fast they drive the pot, not
in how hot the meat gets. Third, **the braise is a savoury-extraction method**: the three records
produce three different sauces from the same cut — a flour-thickened pan gravy (Wyoming), a
stock-and-wine reduction (Food Network) and a hominy-thickened, cedar-and-agave braise (Sherman) —
which is why the cut's lean, mineral flavour responds better here than in the oven. The one
shared craft point is browning before the liquid goes in: every recipe sears first, and the
Sherman record gives it the most time (15 minutes) because the liquid that follows carries
smoke-adjacent cedar and sage rather than a wine reduction.

## Summary

Braise a bison roast in a covered pot after a hard sear, at anywhere from 180 °F for eight hours
to 300 °F for two and a half — the liquid clamps the meat near boiling either way, so the oven
setting buys speed, not doneness. All three sources finish on texture: the meat must give way
under a fork, around 93 °C internally by house measure. Brown first, deglaze with wine or stock,
keep the lid on, and use the braising liquid as the sauce. A probe is useful here for knowing
when you are close, and useless as a definition of done.
