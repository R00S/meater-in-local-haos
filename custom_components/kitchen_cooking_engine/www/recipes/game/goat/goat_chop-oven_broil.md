# Goat Chop × Oven Broil — Recipe Temperature Research

## Cut profile

Broiling is the oven's answer to a grill, and for a small lean chop it solves the same problem
from the other side: intense direct radiant heat on the top face, no pan full of fat, and a cook
measured in minutes. A goat loin or rib chop is 2–3 cm thick and 100–150 g, thin enough that the
broiler can bring the centre up before the surface dries — which is precisely what makes the
method viable for a meat this lean. What it cannot do is brown the underside, so the two records
here arrange the heat differently: one puts the chops on a rack under the element for a straight
ten minutes and calls it medium-rare, the other bakes at a moderate 350 °F first and only then
finishes under the broiler, four minutes a side. Carryover is brief — the chops come out of a hot
oven and go straight to a warm plate — and both records rest between five minutes and the time it
takes to steam a vegetable.

## Source recipes

### 1. My Heart Beets (Ashley, US/Indian-American) — Broiled Goat Loin Chops
**Inspired by**: https://myheartbeets.com/broiled-goat-chops/ (fetched 2026-10-26, published 2015-03-25). My Heart Beets (US food blog; the author's parents grew up in India and the recipe is seasoned Indian-style). Indian-American home tradition — **Culinary Group A** source. **Label flag**: this record calls **145 °F "medium-rare"** — the US pork-era figure rather than the modern beef-convention label; see the consensus below.
**Serves**: not stated (about 6 small chops) · **Prep**: 5 min (+overnight marinating if possible) · **Cook**: 10 min · **Rest**: 5 min

**Ingredients**
- 450 g (1 lb) goat loin chops, about 6 small chops
- 3 tbsp avocado oil, or fat of choice
- ½ tsp salt, adjust to taste
- ½ tsp black pepper
- ½ tsp garlic powder
- ½ tsp amchur (*amchur* — dried mango powder, souring agent)
- ¼ tsp cayenne
- ¼ tsp ground cumin

**Method**
1. Put the chops in a bowl with all the other ingredients and mix well. Marinate **overnight if possible**; otherwise proceed.
2. Bring the chops to **room temperature** before broiling — the source's only preparation note.
3. Set a small **wire rack over a foil-lined pan** and lay the chops on it.
4. Turn on the broiler. Broil the chops **4–5 inches from the heat source for ten minutes**.
5. **Pull at 63 °C (145 °F)** — **the source's own figure**: "The goat should register 145 °F using a meat thermometer and will be medium-rare."
6. Rest **5 minutes** before eating. Final serving temperature: **not stated beyond the 145 °F**.
7. The page's own positioning of the method, quoted: loin and rib chops "are the perfect cuts for broiling, grilling or searing over the stove-top in a cast iron skillet", whereas shoulder and leg need slow cooking.

### 2. Full Circle Farm / Cypress Consultants (Alicia Stoltzfoos, US) — Baked Goat Chops with Fresh Basil
**Inspired by**: https://fullcirclerealfoods.com/recipes/baked-goat-chops-with-fresh-basil (fetched 2026-10-26, published 2019-07-25). Full Circle Farm (US grass-fed meat farm). US farm-kitchen tradition — **Culinary Group B** source. **Quantity flag**: the seasoning is given "to taste" with only the chops (4) and the basil (¼ cup) measured, so this record does not meet the full-quantity rule; it is counted for its two-stage oven-broil method and flagged here.
**Serves**: 4 chops · **Prep**: 5 min · **Cook**: 38 min · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 4 goat chops
- ¼ cup chopped sweet basil
- Coarse sea salt, to taste
- Black pepper, to taste
- Dehydrated minced onion, to taste
- Garlic powder, to taste

**Method**
1. Preheat the oven to **350 °F (177 °C)**.
2. Combine the salt, pepper, onion and garlic powder in a small bowl and rub onto both sides of the chops.
3. Put the chops on a baking sheet and spread the chopped basil evenly over each one.
4. Bake **30 minutes**.
5. **At the end of baking, run the chops under the broiler for 8 minutes — 4 minutes on each side.**
6. There is **no temperature in this record**; the signals are the two stage times and the basil crust.
7. **Pull at 68 °C (155 °F)** — house guidance, **not the source's number**: 30 minutes at 350 °F plus eight minutes of broiling takes a 2.5 cm chop past the pink stage, and the house figure is chosen to match the method's likely overshoot rather than the other records' 54–57 °C. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.
8. The page notes the chops "can also be grilled or even pan fried" with the same seasoning.

## Temperature consensus

| Source | Tradition | Oven and time | Published internal temperature |
|---|---|---|---|
| My Heart Beets (Indian-American) | Indian-style dry rub, broiler only | **broiler**, 4–5 in from element, 10 min | **145 °F (63 °C)**, labelled "medium-rare" |
| Full Circle Farm (US) | US farm kitchen, bake then broil | **350 °F** 30 min + **broiler** 8 min (4/side) | None — stage times only |

One measured row and one silent one, and they point in different directions — which is the honest
finding of this leaf. My Heart Beets publishes **145 °F** and calls it medium-rare; the cut's other
two leaves have sources pulling at **130–135 °F** and calling that medium-rare to medium-well. On
the US convention that most of this corpus follows, 145 °F is *medium*, which is also the USDA
safe minimum for whole cuts of goat — so the same number is being used as a preferred eating
temperature by one writer and as a safety floor by another. The Full Circle record has no number
and a method (30 minutes at 350 °F plus broiling) that will almost certainly land above it.
Mapping to the engine: the measured 63 °C sits at the top of the `medium` band (57–63 °C), and
`recommended_doneness` for this method is therefore **`medium`** — the same direction as the
corpus, one step above the sear and grill leaves. This leaf is the one place where the cut's
`well_done` recommendation and the corpus come closest, and it is still two bands apart.

## What makes this method special

The broiler is the only appliance here that cooks a chop from **above** at a temperature high
enough to brown it while leaving the pan clean — no fat to manage, no flare-ups, no grill to heat.
That makes it the most convenient method for this cut and the least forgiving: the top face is
metres-per-second of radiant heat away from burning while the underside is only warmed by the
oven's air, so a chop that is not turned (My Heart Beets doesn't turn it) or not pre-baked (Full
Circle does) develops a one-sided crust. The two records are really two philosophies of the same
appliance: a **straight ten minutes under the element** on a rack, relying on distance and a short
time to keep the inside pink, versus a **two-stage bake-then-broil** that heats the chop through in
moderate air and uses the broiler only for the finish. The seasoning follows the same split — the
Indian dry rub is a marinade that works overnight and caramelises under the flame, while the basil
is laid on top as a protective crust that would scorch if it went under the broiler any earlier.
Both records agree on one practical point: a rack or a baking sheet with a lip, never a deep pan.

## Summary

Broiling is the fastest way to cook a goat chop at home: ten minutes four inches under a hot
element on a rack, with an overnight dry rub, and it is done at 145 °F. The alternative is a
two-stage cook — 350 °F for thirty minutes to heat through, then eight minutes under the broiler,
four a side — which browns both faces but will take a thin chop past pink. Season strongly, use a
rack, and rest five minutes. Do not walk away: the broiler's margin on a lean chop is seconds.
