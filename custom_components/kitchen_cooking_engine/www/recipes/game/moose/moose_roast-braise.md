# Moose Roast × Braise — Recipe Temperature Research

## Cut profile

Moose (*Alces alces* — *älg* in Swedish, *elg* in Norwegian, *Elch* in German) is the largest deer, and its
haunch and shoulder roasts carry the lowest fat cover of any red meat in this catalogue: roughly 1–3 % total
fat with almost none between the muscle groups, so there is no basting layer between the oven air and the
meat. That single fact is why braising suits the cut better than logic suggests. A dry oven drives water off
a lean roast faster than conduction can replace it, and the result is a grey, dry band two centimetres deep
around an undercooked centre; a closed pot with a splash of wine and stock replaces that lost surface water
continuously, so the whole roast can be brought slowly to serving temperature without a gradient.

The second difference from beef is structural, and it changes the target temperature. A beef chuck or short
rib is held at 88–96 °C for hours to hydrolyse dense collagen; a moose haunch has far less collagen and very
fine fibres, so the same treatment that rescues a short rib destroys this roast. Every tradition surveyed
here pulls it between 63 and 70 °C instead — a range that belongs to the doneness bands of a steak, not to a
braise, which is the most interesting finding in this leaf.

Carryover in a covered pot is small but real: 3–5 °C while the roast rests in its liquid, because the pot
keeps feeding heat through the base. Probe from the thick end into the centre of the largest muscle mass,
clear of the bone and of the silvery seam that separates the topside from the silverside — both read low.

## Styckesprofil

Älgstek är det magraste röda köttet i den här katalogen — 1–3 % fett och nästan inget mellan musklerna — så
det finns inget fettlager som skyddar mot ugnsluften. Bräsering i sluten gryta med vin och buljong ersätter
den ytvattenförlust som annars ger en grå, torr rand runt en underkokt kärna. Skillnaden mot nötkött är
strukturell: en älgstek har lite kollagen och fina fibrer, så den ska inte upp till 90 °C som ett revbensspjäll
utan dras vid 63–70 °C. Eftervärme i gryta: 3–5 °C. Sond i den tjockaste muskeln, borta från ben och
silversena.

## Source recipes

### 1. Swedish home kitchen — Bräserad älgstek med rödvin (inspired by Tobbe, Tobbes kök)
**Inspired by**: http://tobbeskok.blogspot.com/2012/11/braserad-algstek-med-graddsas-och.html
**Serves**: 10 · **Prep**: 15 min · **Cook**: 2–3 hr · **Rest**: 15 min

**Ingredients**
- 1.6 kg moose roast (*älgstek*), tied
- 500 ml dry red wine
- 2 carrots, peeled
- 2 yellow onions, peeled and halved
- Salt and freshly ground black pepper, generously
- For the sauce: 500 ml whipping cream, 1–2 tbsp blackcurrant jelly, 1 tbsp butter, the pan juices

**Method**
1. Salt and pepper the roast heavily — the source is explicit that the meat "needs a lot of both" because it
   is so lean.
2. Brown the roast on high heat in a frying pan until it has good colour on all sides.
3. Heat the oven to 120 °C. Put the roast in an ovenproof dish, pour the wine over it and add the onion and
   carrot.
4. Insert a roasting thermometer into the thickest part of the roast, avoiding bone and the seam between
   muscles, and put the dish in the oven.
5. Cook 2–3 hours. The source's own scale: **60 °C** is red, **65 °C** is pink (*rosa*), **70 °C** is
   well done (*välstekt*).
6. **Pull at 65 °C** (149 °F) for the pink centre the Swedish tradition serves, or 70 °C (158 °F) if the
   table prefers no red.
7. Cover the dish with foil and let it stand until serving — the source rests it in the dish, not on a board.
8. Strain the pan juices into a saucepan, boil up, add the cream and finish with blackcurrant jelly, salt and
   pepper.
9. Carve in centimetre-thick slices. Final serving temperature: **67 °C / 153 °F** (pink, *rosa*).

### 2. Norwegian — Elgstek i leirgryte (inspired by Godt.no / Opplysningskontoret for egg og kjøtt)
**Inspired by**: https://www.godt.no/oppskrifter/kjoett/vilt/3700/elgstek-i-leirgryte
**Serves**: 8 · **Prep**: 20 min (+2 hr clay-pot soaking) · **Cook**: 2–3 hr · **Rest**: 15 min

**Ingredients**
- 1.5 kg moose roast (*elgstek*)
- 2 tsp salt
- 1 tsp freshly ground black pepper
- 1 tsp dried thyme
- 1 tsp dried oregano
- 2 tbsp butter, in knobs
- 1 onion, in wedges
- 1 carrot, in chunks
- 400 ml meat stock
- For the sauce: 2 tbsp unsalted butter, 2 tbsp plain flour, 100 ml sour cream (*seterrømme*),
  1 tbsp rowanberry jelly (*rognebærgelé*), salt and pepper

**Method**
1. Soak the clay pot (*leirgryte*) in water for a couple of hours — a dry pot cracks when it meets the heat.
2. Rub the roast all over with salt and the dried herbs and put it in the pot with the onion, carrot and
   knobs of butter.
3. Insert a roasting thermometer into the thickest part of the roast, clear of bone.
4. Put the pot into a **cold** oven and then set it to 125 °C. Cook 2–3 hours.
5. The source's targets: **65 °C** is medium (*medium stekt*), **70 °C** is fully cooked
   (*gjennomstekt*).
6. **Pull at 65 °C** (149 °F) — or 70 °C (158 °F) — and rest the roast at least a quarter of an hour
   before carving.
7. Strain the juices, brown butter and flour, add the juices and whisk in the sour cream; season with the
   rowanberry jelly.
8. Final serving temperature: **67–68 °C / 153–154 °F** (medium, faintly pink).

### 3. Canadian / British Columbian — Dutch Oven Moose Roast (inspired by All-About-Moose.com)
**Inspired by**: https://www.all-about-moose.com/dutch-oven-moose-roast.html
**Serves**: 6 · **Prep**: 20 min · **Cook**: 6 hr · **Rest**: 10 min

**Ingredients**
- 1.25 kg sirloin-tip moose roast
- 1 medium onion, chopped
- 4 garlic cloves, crushed
- 2 tbsp coconut oil (or oil of preference)
- 240 ml beef stock
- 120 ml red wine
- 4 slices bacon
- 2 celery sticks
- 2 carrots
- Salt and black pepper
- Fresh parsley, rosemary, sage and thyme, a few sprigs each

**Method**
1. Preheat the oven to 250 °F (121 °C).
2. Heat the oil in a 5-quart Dutch oven on the stovetop over medium-high heat and brown the roast on all
   sides; set it aside.
3. Soften the onion in the same pot, return the roast, spoon onion over it, smear the crushed garlic on top
   and lay the bacon slices over the meat.
4. Pour the stock and wine slowly over the roast, arrange the carrots and celery around it and lay the herbs
   on top.
5. Put the lid on, simmer on the stovetop for about 15 minutes to heat the pot through, then transfer it,
   covered, to the oven.
6. Insert a meat thermometer into the centre of the roast before it goes in — the source checks the roast
   with a thermometer and names **145 °F (63 °C)** as the minimum it must reach.
7. Cook 6 hours. **Pull at 63 °C** (145 °F) as the source's floor; by the time it reaches it, the roast is
   falling apart rather than sliceable.
8. Rest 10 minutes, then reduce the braising liquid to a gravy. Final serving temperature:
   **66 °C / 151 °F** (fall-apart, well done by North American game standards).

### 4. Russian — Тушёная лосятина (inspired by Екатерина Фесенко, Povar.ru)
**Inspired by**: https://m.povar.ru/recipes/tushenaya_losyatina-53915.html
**Serves**: 4 · **Prep**: 20 min (+2–3 days soaking, +3–4 hr marinating) · **Cook**: 2–2.5 hr · **Rest**: 10 min

**Ingredients**
- 1 kg moose meat from the roast (*лосятина*), cut into large pieces
- 100 ml dry white wine
- 1 tbsp mustard
- 1 tbsp vegetable oil
- 4–6 onions, sliced
- Spices to taste (the source seasons with "your favourite spices" and does not salt — moose carries
  enough natural salt for the author)
- 2 tbsp sour cream, optional, at the end

**Method**
1. Soak the meat in cold water for 2–3 days, changing the water regularly — the Russian technique for
   drawing out strong game flavour before cooking.
2. Beat the meat lightly with a mallet and cut it into pieces. Marinate 3–4 hours with the wine, mustard and
   spices.
3. Heat the oil and brown the meat, then add the sliced onions.
4. Pour in water to cover the meat and leave it to *томиться* — barely tremble — on the lowest heat for
   2–2.5 hours.
5. Finish with a couple of spoonfuls of sour cream if you like.
6. The source publishes **no internal temperature** and judges the pot by fork tenderness: nothing on the
   page tells a probe user what to look for, so no pull temperature can be quoted without inventing one.
7. Final serving temperature: **not published** — serve straight from the pot, fork-tender.

*Note: this source braises pieces of the roast rather than the whole muscle, so it is a pot braise of the same
cut rather than a whole-roast braise. Included because it is the only fetched Russian recipe with full
quantities, and because the tradition's silence on temperature is itself a data point.*

## Temperature consensus

| Tradition | Source | Pot / vessel | Oven or heat | Pull temperature | Final serving temp |
|-----------|--------|--------------|--------------|------------------|--------------------|
| Swedish home | Tobbe, Tobbes kök (2012) | Open oven dish, wine | 120 °C | **65 °C** pink / 70 °C well done | 67 °C |
| Norwegian | Godt.no / Opplysningskontoret for egg og kjøtt | Clay pot, lidded | 125 °C, cold start | **65 °C** medium / 70 °C well done | 67–68 °C |
| Canadian | All-About-Moose.com (BC) | Dutch oven, lidded | 250 °F (121 °C) | **63 °C** stated minimum | 66 °C |
| Russian | Екатерина Фесенко, Povar.ru | Stovetop pot, lidded | lowest flame | **not published** | not published |

The three numeric sources bracket **63–70 °C**, and the two Nordic sources agree exactly: 65 °C is the
pink/medium target and 70 °C is fully cooked. That is a *doneness* disagreement with the cut's own coding,
not between the cooks — every one of them is cooking this roast like a steak, not like a beef short rib. The
Canadian number is different in kind: 145 °F is the North American minimum internal temperature for whole-muscle
red meat, and the author applies it to game as a floor rather than choosing it as a doneness preference. The
Russian source is the honest counter-example of the set — it braises for two and a half hours and never
mentions a thermometer at all.

The practical reading for a probe: a moose roast in a covered pot will sit at 60–63 °C for a long time on its
way to 65 °C because the pot is at 120–125 °C, so the last three degrees are slow and the roast must be
watched rather than timed. Pulling at 65 °C and resting under the lid lands at 67–68 °C, which is what both
Nordic sources serve.

## What makes this method special

Braising a moose roast is a controlled-moisture problem rather than a collagen problem. The cut has so little
fat that evaporative loss from an open oven is the dominant failure mode: water leaves the surface faster
than heat conducts inward, the outer centimetres dry and toughen, and the roast loses weight it cannot
afford. A lid, a clay pot or a foil cover changes the boundary condition entirely — the air around the meat
saturates, evaporation stops, and heat moves in slowly and evenly. Wine adds acidity and small amounts of
dissolved solids that brown into the sauce, and the pot liquor becomes the sauce in every one of the four
traditions above. What braising does *not* need to do here is hydrolyse collagen, which is why the targets sit
at 63–70 °C instead of the 88–96 °C a beef short rib demands.

## Summary

Braising suits the moose roast because its 1–3 % fat leaves nothing to protect it from dry oven air, and a
lidded pot replaces the surface water an open oven removes. All three sources that publish numbers pull
between 63 and 70 °C, treating the roast as a lean steak rather than a collagen cut, and the two Nordic
sources agree exactly on 65 °C pink / 70 °C fully cooked. The Russian source braises the same cut for two and
a half hours with no thermometer at all, which is the tradition-level disagreement this leaf records rather
than resolves.
