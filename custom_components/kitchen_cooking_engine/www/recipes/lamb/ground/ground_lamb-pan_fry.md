## Cut profile

Pan-frying ground lamb is a two-beat technique in both fetched sources: a hard sear to brown
the mince (or shaped cutlets) and drive off surface moisture — the Maillard step that
separates grey mince from savoury mince — followed by a gentler covered phase to cook the
interior through. Because grinding distributes surface bacteria through the meat, both the
food-safety logic and the sources demand the mince be cooked *completely*, not pink. The
fetched sources differ in form (Russian shaped cutlets of home-ground lamb-and-beef vs Indian
keema filling browned loose) but share the sear-then-cover architecture, and one of them
publishes the collection's only stated internal endpoint for this cut.

## Source recipes

### 1. Котлеты из баранины и говядины (Lamb and beef cutlets) — Нина Минина-Р / iamcook.ru
**Inspired by**: https://www.iamcook.ru/showrecipe/27040 (fetched 2026-10-24). Nina Minina-R, iamcook.ru; Russian tradition. Note as published: the mince is home-ground from boneless lamb (300 g) and beef (200 g) — mixed-meat cutlets, not package lamb mince.

**Serves**: not stated · **Prep**: not stated · **Cook**: 20 min stated (1–2 min/side sear + ~15 min covered) · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- Boneless lamb (б/к) — 300 g
- Boneless beef (б/к) — 200 g
- Onion — about 50 g
- Garlic — 1–2 cloves
- Salt — up to 1/2 teaspoon
- Black pepper — 0.3 teaspoon
- Dried herbs — to taste
- Oil — for frying

**Method** *(translated faithfully)*
1. Cut the lamb and beef into pieces, then grind them in a meat grinder.
2. Grind the onion into the mince; add the chopped garlic, black pepper and salt.
3. Knead the mixture until uniform and sticky; the author also recommends beating the mince by throwing it against the work surface or bowl.
4. Divide into equal portions. With wet hands, shape oval or round cutlets about 2 cm thick.
5. Heat oil in a frying pan. Fry both sides over **high heat** until browned, **1–2 minutes per side**.
6. Reduce the heat to moderate or low, **cover the pan**, and fry until done, about **15 minutes**, turning 2–4 more times.

**Probe and endpoint**: no probe step. **no internal temperature stated** — the endpoint is "until done" after the covered phase.

### 2. Keema Naan filling — Swasthi Shreekanth / Indian Healthy Recipes
**Inspired by**: https://www.indianhealthyrecipes.com/keema-naan/ (fetched 2026-10-24; the URL redirects from the previously recorded /keema/ address). Swasthi Shreekanth, Indian Healthy Recipes; Indian tradition. The page says "I use lamb keema here"; filling: "250 grams (or ½ lb) keema (minced or ground meat lamb/goat)". The pan-cooked keema filling is the ground-lamb preparation of record here.

**Serves**: 6 naans (filling) · **Prep**: 15 min (+2 h dough fermentation) · **Cook**: 35 min total · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients** *(filling only)*
- 250 g (1/2 lb) keema (minced/ground lamb or goat)
- 2 tbsp oil
- 1/2 cup chopped onion (1 small onion)
- 1 chopped green chili
- 1 1/2 tsp ginger-garlic paste
- 1/4 tsp turmeric
- 1/2 tsp Kashmiri red chili powder
- 1/2 tsp garam masala
- 1 tsp coriander powder (optional)
- 1 tsp cumin powder
- 1 tsp tomato paste (or 1/2 tsp amchur/chaat masala/lemon juice, optional)
- 1/2 tsp sea salt
- 1 tsp kasuri methi (optional)
- 1/4 cup finely chopped coriander leaves

**Method** *(filling phase)*
1. Heat the oil in a pan; add the onions and green chilies. Sauté until light golden, **6–7 minutes**.
2. Add the ginger and garlic; sauté a minute. Add the ground spices.
3. Deglaze with 2 tbsp water; add the keema and **sauté 4–5 minutes over medium-high heat**.
4. Add 1/4 cup hot water and **cook covered** until tender and fully cooked, adjusting the hot water as needed.
5. If using tomato paste, dilute it with 2 tbsp water and add it with the salt and kasuri methi. Cook until the moisture evaporates.
6. Turn off the heat; cool completely and mix in the coriander leaves.

**Probe and endpoint**: the page gives a doneness figure for the filling — the keema is cooked when it reaches **161 ℉ / 72 ℃**.
Final serving temperature: **72 °C / 161 °F** (as stated by the source; no separate carryover figure given).

## Temperature consensus

| Tradition | Preferred doneness name | Final serving temp |
|-----------|------------------------|--------------------|
| Russian (iamcook / Nina Minina-R, lamb-beef cutlets) | "fry until done" — 15 min covered | Not stated |
| Indian (Indian Healthy Recipes / Swasthi, keema filling) | "cooked when it reaches 161 ℉ or 72 ℃" | 72 °C / 161 °F |

The full observed range across the fetched sources where temperatures are stated is a single
value — **72 °C / 161 °F** — with one source silent and cooking to "done". The stated figure
sits just above the cut file's `well_done` target (71 °C / 160 °F, the ground-meat safe line)
and is the collection's only measured ground-lamb endpoint. The divergence in this method is
about form and fat, not doneness: shaped cutlets of mixed home-ground meats stay juicy through
a covered fry, while loose keema is browned hard and finished covered to drive off moisture.
Both sources require complete cooking — no source treats ground lamb as a pink-cooking meat.

## What makes this method special

Pan-frying is where ground lamb earns its flavour: the sear generates Maillard products across
the enormous surface area of the mince, and the fat (typically 15–25 %) fries the aromatics —
onion, garlic, cumin — in rendered lamb fat rather than neutral oil. The grinding that makes
the cut versatile also makes it the least tolerant of undercooking in the lamb family, which
is why both fetched sources finish covered: the lid holds steam that cooks every particle
through while the browning already happened. For shaped cutlets the sear sets the crust before
the covered phase; for loose mince it is simply stirred and browned in batches.

## Summary

Pan-frying ground lamb is a sear-and-cover operation that builds savoury browned crust while guaranteeing the mince cooks completely — the one absolute this cut demands. Russian cutlets and Indian keema show the same architecture in two forms, and the keema source supplies the collection's only measured endpoint at 72 °C. The method is the cut's default for good reason: maximum browning, minimum risk.
