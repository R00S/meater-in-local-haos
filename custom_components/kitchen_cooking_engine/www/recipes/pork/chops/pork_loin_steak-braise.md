# Pork Loin Steak × Braise — Recipe Temperature Research

## Cut profile

Braising surrounds the steak with a shallow, simmering liquid: the meat is seared for crust
first, then held below a boil in sauce until connective tissue — of which this cut has very
little — and time do what dry heat cannot. The fetched sources use two configurations: an
American skillet braise where the chops simmer 45–60 minutes in a broth-brown-sugar-balsamic
sauce (stovetop covered, or 163 °C/325 °F oven), and a Chinese *hóngshāo* red-braise where
thin chops are starch-coated, fried at 160 °C and then simmered 1–2 minutes in a soy-ginger
glaze. The method is atypical for a lean loin steak — the long wet cook that rescues shoulder
or belly will dry out a uniform white-muscle steak if pushed — which is why the American
source's window (45–60 min) reads long for this cut and the Chinese source keeps its simmer
to minutes. No source states an internal temperature; both endpoints are time-and-sauce
judged. Carryover is negligible in liquid.

## Source recipes

### 1. Valerie's Kitchen — Skillet Braised Pork Chops
**Inspired by**: https://www.fromvalerieskitchen.com/skillet-braised-pork-chops/ (fetched 2026-10-26). Valerie's Kitchen (US food publisher). Recipe explicitly names bone-in pork loin chops.
**Serves**: 4 · **Prep**: 5 min · **Cook**: 1 h 10 min · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 3 tbsp olive oil, divided, or as needed
- 4 bone-in pork loin chops
- Kosher salt and freshly ground black pepper
- 2 tsp chopped fresh thyme
- 1 cup diced onions; 2 cloves garlic, minced
- 1 cup low-sodium beef broth
- 3 tbsp light brown sugar; 2 tbsp balsamic vinegar
- 1 tbsp Dijon mustard; 1 tbsp Worcestershire sauce

**Method**
1. Heat 2 tbsp olive oil in a heavy skillet over medium-high; brown the chops **about 3–4 minutes per side**, seasoning with salt, pepper and thyme. Remove.
2. Drain the grease; add the remaining tablespoon of oil; reduce heat to medium; cook the onions **about 1 minute**, then add the garlic and cook until the onions soften and lightly brown.
3. Add the broth, brown sugar, balsamic vinegar, Dijon and Worcestershire; stir **about 1 minute**.
4. Return the chops to the skillet and nestle them into the sauce; bring to a boil, cover, reduce to low and cook **45 minutes to 1 hour**, until fork-tender.
5. Oven option: after searing and adding the chops to sauce, cover and braise at **325 °F** for **45–55 minutes**.
6. The source publishes no internal temperature; doneness is fork-tenderness plus time. House guidance (not the source's): probe the thickest chop — **Pull at 63 °C (145 °F)** if keeping the steak itself medium. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.

### 2. China Sichuan Food — Red-braised Pork Chops (红烧猪排)
**Inspired by**: https://www.chinasichuanfood.com/red-braised-pork-chops/ (fetched 2026-10-26). China Sichuan Food (Yulin Ouyang, Chinese-food publisher). **Cut-form flag**: the source specifies chops about **1 cm thick** — thinner than this leaf's 2–3 cm steak form; recorded as the Chinese red-braise technique applied to thinner chops, not passed off as a thick-steak recipe.
**Serves**: 4 with steamed rice · **Prep**: 20 min · **Cook**: 20 min · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 4 pork chops, around 1 cm thick
- 4 tbsp starch (cornstarch or sweet potato starch); 1½ tbsp water
- 2 green onions, cut into 1-inch sections; 1 green onion, finely chopped
- 1 small thumb ginger, sliced
- 2 tbsp light soy sauce; ½ tsp sugar
- Oil for frying
- **Marinade**: 1 tbsp light soy sauce; ½ tbsp rice wine; ⅛ tsp white pepper; small pinch of salt

**Method**
1. Pound the chops with a meat pounder or the back of a knife; marinate with the rice wine, white pepper, salt and light soy sauce for **10–15 minutes**.
2. Mix the 4 tbsp starch with 1½ tbsp water; coat the chops.
3. Heat the frying oil to **160 °C** and fry the chops individually until browned.
4. Heat about 1 tbsp oil in a pan; fry the ginger and green onion over low heat until aromatic.
5. Add the chops, a small pinch of salt, sugar and light soy sauce; **simmer 1–2 minutes**.
6. Remove the chops; discard the ginger and green onion; pour the sauce over the chops and garnish with finely chopped green onion.
7. The source publishes no internal temperature; the endpoint is the 1–2 minute simmer. House guidance (not the source's): this thin form cooks through inside the simmer — no probe is used in the source's tradition. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.

## What makes this method special

Braising is the only method here that cooks the steak in a medium it can absorb: the sear
builds crust, then the liquid — acidic and sugary in both sources — carries flavour into the
flesh while gentle heat works from all sides at once. For a lean cut with little collagen the
physics is a warning rather than a gift: there is no connective tissue to melt into gelatin,
so every minute beyond doneness converts moisture into evaporation and the sauce's long simmer
becomes a race against dryness, which is exactly why the Chinese technique keeps its simmer to
minutes and pounds thin, while the American source's hour-long braise targets fork-tenderness
rather than a probe reading. The two sauces do opposite jobs on the surface — the balsamic-
mustard glaze reduces and clings after the meat is already browned, the soy-ginger glaze
lacquers thin starch-coated chops during their brief finish. The method's real value for this
cut is flavour transfer and forgiveness in the short window, not the long cook its name
implies.

## Summary

Braising cooks a pork loin steak in simmering sauce — the American source for the better part
of an hour, the Chinese red-braise for a minute or two — and neither probes the meat at all.
It is an unusual match for this cut: the lean, uniform loin has no collagen to convert and
dries if left in liquid too long, so the method trades the steak's juiciness for deep sauce
flavour. Use it when the dish is about the sauce and the meat can afford to be tender rather
than rosy, and keep the wet phase short on thick steaks.
