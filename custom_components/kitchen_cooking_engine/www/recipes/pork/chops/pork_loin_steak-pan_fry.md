# Pork Loin Steak × Pan Fry — Recipe Temperature Research

## Cut profile

Pan frying is the native method for this cut: a lean, uniform cross-section from the
*longissimus dorsi* has no connective tissue to break down and no bone to conduct heat
unevenly, so all it needs is direct conductive heat fast enough to brown the surface before
the centre dries. The fetched sources run 1.5–4 minutes per side in butter or oil at
medium-high to high heat, three of the five baste with butter, and the observed pull band is
62–65 °C — the lean white-muscle flesh gives very little warning between juicy and dry, so
the window is short and the carryover (2–4 °C) matters more than on a fattier chop. The
method suits the cut's shape: a flat 2–3 cm steak lies flush in the pan and colours evenly
edge to edge.

## Styckesprofil

Pannstekning är den ursprungliga metoden för detta stycke: ett mager, enhetligt tvärsnitt från
*longissimus dorsi* saknar bindväv att bryta ner och ben som leder värma ojämnt, så allt som

form: en platt 2,5–3,5 cm steke ligger platt i pannan och färgas jämnt från kant till kant.

## Source recipes

### 1. Madens Verden — Pandestegning af koteletter (pan-fried pork chops, technique applied to loin steak)
**Inspired by**: https://madensverden.dk/pandestegning-af-koteletter/ (fetched 2026-04-24). Holger Rørby Madsen, Madens Verden (Danish food publisher/cookbook author). Danish two-phase butter-baste technique; the leaf applies it to the boneless loin steak form.
**Serves**: 2 · **Prep**: 20 min salting · **Cook**: 8 min · **Rest**: 5 min (stated in method)

**Ingredients**
- 2 pork loin steaks, 150–200 g each
- Generous knob of butter
- 2 garlic cloves, rosemary, thyme
- Salt (20 min ahead), black pepper

**Method**
1. Salt the steaks 20 minutes ahead; pat dry.
2. Heat the pan to high; add butter; let it foam.
3. Sear **2 minutes per side** on high without moving.
4. Reduce heat; add garlic and herbs; baste continuously for **2 more minutes per side**.
5. Season with pepper; rest 5 minutes.
6. House guidance (not the source's): insert the probe from the side toward the geometric centre — **Pull at 62–63 °C (144–145 °F)**. Final serving temperature: **63–65 °C (145–149 °F)** (source's stated core band).

### 2. ichkoche.at — Schweinskotelett Grundrezept (adapted for boneless loin steak)
**Inspired by**: https://www.ichkoche.at/schweinskotelett-rezept-236782 (fetched 2026-04-24). ichkoche.at (Austrian recipe platform). Austrian fundamentals: very hot dry pan, then oil, butter-herb baste.
**Serves**: 2 · **Prep**: not stated · **Cook**: 5 min · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 2 pork loin steaks
- Neutral oil
- 1–2 garlic cloves, 1 rosemary sprig, 3–4 thyme sprigs
- 1 tbsp butter
- Salt and pepper

**Method**
1. Heat the pan dry until very hot; add oil.
2. Sear the loin steaks **2 minutes undisturbed** on the first side.
3. Turn; add garlic, herbs and butter; baste **2 more minutes**.
4. Remove; season; serve immediately.
5. House guidance (not the source's): probe the centre — **Core: 63–65 °C (145–149 °F)** (the source's stated core band). Final serving temperature: **63–65 °C (145–149 °F)**.

### 3. Directo al Paladar — Chuleta de cerdo a la plancha
**Inspired by**: https://www.directoalpaladar.com/recetas-de-carnes-y-aves/como-hacer-mejores-chuletas-cerdo-a-plancha-jugosas-churruscaditas (fetched 2026-04-24). Directo al Paladar (Spanish food media). The Spanish griddle method: temper, short contact, salt after.
**Serves**: 1–2 · **Prep**: 15 min · **Cook**: 3 min · **Rest**: 1 min (stated in method)

**Ingredients**
- 2 pork loin steaks
- A few drops olive oil (on the meat)
- Flaked salt, black pepper (added after cooking)

**Method**
1. Remove the steaks from the fridge 15 minutes ahead; rub with drops of olive oil.
2. Heat the griddle pan to medium-high.
3. Cook **1.5 minutes per side** undisturbed — the source's rule is to respect the temperature of the meat and the pan.
4. Season with flaked salt and pepper; rest 1 minute; serve.
5. House guidance (not the source's): probe the centre — **Core: 63–65 °C (145–149 °F)** (the source's stated core band). Final serving temperature: **63–65 °C (145–149 °F)**.

### 4. Just One Cookbook — Tonteki トンテキ (Mie-style garlic-soy pork)
**Inspired by**: https://www.justonecookbook.com/tonteki/ (fetched 2026-10-26). Just One Cookbook, Namiko Hirasawa Chen (Japanese-food publisher); the page presents the dish as a Mie Prefecture regional specialty popularised by *Midnight Diner: Tokyo Stories*.
**Serves**: 2 · **Prep**: 15 min · **Cook**: 10 min · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 2 boneless pork loin chops, ½ inch (1.3 cm) thick, ½ lb (227 g)
- ¼ yellow onion (core intact, for grating)
- ⅛ tsp Diamond Crystal kosher salt; ⅛ tsp freshly ground black pepper
- 1 tbsp all-purpose flour; 1 tbsp neutral oil
- **Sauce**: 3 tbsp ponzu; 3 tbsp sake; ½ tbsp soy sauce
- Optional garnish: ¼ head green cabbage, 1 tomato, parsley

**Method**
1. Grate the onion; shred the cabbage; cut the tomato into wedges.
2. Whisk the ponzu, sake and soy sauce together.
3. Pat the chops dry; slit the connective tissue between meat and fat on both sides so they stay flat.
4. Season one side with salt and pepper; coat in flour and shake off the excess.
5. Heat a frying pan over medium-high with neutral oil; add the chops and cook **4 minutes per side**, waiting until the underside is golden before flipping.
6. Add the sauce and grated onion; spoon sauce over the meat to coat.
7. Slice into bite-size pieces if serving with chopsticks; serve with cabbage and tomato.
8. The source publishes no internal temperature; doneness is time plus colour. House guidance (not the source's): probe the centre — **Pull at 63 °C (145 °F)** (USDA-aligned line for this cut). Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.

### 5. Hint of Healthy — Pan seared pork loin steaks
**Inspired by**: https://www.hintofhealthy.com/pan-seared-pork-loin-steaks/ (fetched 2026-10-26). Hint of Healthy, Tonje (Norwegian-born, UK-based food publisher; page labels the cuisine international).
**Serves**: 4 steaks · **Prep**: 1 min · **Cook**: 10 min · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 4 pork loin steaks
- Salt and pepper, to taste
- ½ tsp dried sage
- 1 tbsp butter

**Method**
1. Season both sides of the steaks generously with salt, pepper and a little dried sage.
2. Melt the butter in a pan over medium to high heat; wait for it to bubble.
3. Add the steaks one or two at a time without overcrowding; cook until the underside begins to brown, flip once and cook the other side — the source says this amounts to **4–5 minutes per side**.
4. The source publishes no internal temperature for cooking; the only number on the page (75 °C / 165 °F) is explicitly for reheating leftovers. House guidance (not the source's): probe the centre — **Pull at 63 °C (145 °F)**. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.

## What makes this method special

A pan is the fastest heat-transfer path this cut will ever meet: butter-conducted conductive heat moves roughly an order of magnitude faster than oven air, so the surface reaches Maillard temperatures while the centre is still climbing — which is exactly what a lean, connective-tissue-free steak needs, since it has no collagen to hide behind and every degree of overshoot shows as dryness. The baste that three of five sources use compensates for the cut's low intramuscular fat by carrying flavored fat over the top surface during the short cook, adding richness the muscle cannot supply itself. The two-phase Danish heat pattern (hard sear, then gentler finish) and the Spanish single-blast contact time are two solutions to the same physics problem: get colour fast, get the centre to 63–65 °C before the water in the fibres cooks out. The failure mode is mechanical as well as thermal — a thin, sugar- or flour-coated surface (as in the Japanese tonteki) sticks if the pan is moved too early, which is why every source leaves the steak undisturbed for the first minutes.

## Summary

Pan frying gives a pork loin steak a browned, butter-rich crust while the centre stays in a narrow moist band — the five sources all stop between 62 and 65 °C, or simply count minutes until golden. It is the best match of all the methods here: the cut is flat, lean and uniform, so it cooks as evenly in a pan as anywhere and takes browning without overcooking if you keep the heat high and the time short. The risk is the opposite of a slow method — there is no buffer, so a distracted minute or two turns juicy flesh dry. Use a probe if you want certainty; use the baste if you want flavour the lean muscle cannot provide.
