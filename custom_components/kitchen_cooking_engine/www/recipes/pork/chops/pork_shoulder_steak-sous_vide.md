# Pork Shoulder Steak × Sous Vide — Recipe Temperature Research

## Cut profile

Sous vide removes every gradient: the steak sits in a bath held at one temperature, so the
collagen in the collar converts at a known, constant rate and the fat renders without any
part of the meat ever exceeding the water. The fetched sources use two regimes — a
long, low American pair (24–38 hours at 66–71 °C) aiming at slicing-tender shoulder that
still holds together, and a shorter, hotter German run (about 4 hours at 61 °C) aiming at
maximum tenderness with a grill finish for crust. The disagreement is real and structural:
the American sources want the meat sliceable after a day and a half, the German source
wants it "almost dissolving" after four hours, and the published bath figures differ by up
to 10 °C because of it. None of the three publishes a meat-core pull temperature — every
number on these pages is a bath setting, which is exactly the distinction this leaf must
keep: the water is not the meat. Probe only as confirmation; the bath has already decided
the centre.

## Styckesprofil

Sous vide tar bort varje gradient: steaken ligger i ett bad som hålls vid en temperatur, så
kollagenet i bogen konverterar i en känd, konstant takt och fettet smälter utan att någon
del av köttet någonsin överstiger vattnet. De hämtade källorna använder två regimer — ett
långt, lågt amerikanskt par (24–38 timmar vid 66–71 °C) som siktar på skivbar bog som
ännu håller ihop, och en kortare, hetare tysk körning (omkring 4 timmar vid 61 °C) som
siktar på maximal mörhet med grillavslut för skorpans skull. Delningen är verklig och
strukturell: de amerikanska källorna vill ha köttet skivbart efter ett och ett halvt dygn,
den tyska källan vill ha det "nästan upplöst" efter fyra timmar, och de publicerade
badtserna skiljer sig upp till 10 °C på grund av det. Ingen av de tre publicerar ett
mått för köttets kärntemperatur — varje siffra på dessa sidor är en badinställning, och
det är precis den distinktion detta blad måste hålla: vattnet är inte köttet.

## Source recipes

### 1. DadCooksDinner — Sous Vide Pork Shoulder Steaks with Purple Cabbage Slaw (Mike Vrobel)
**Inspired by**: https://www.dadcooksdinner.com/sous-vide-pork-shoulder-steaks-with-purple-cabbage-slaw/ (fetched 2026-10-26). Mike Vrobel, DadCooksDinner (US).
**Serves**: not stated (2 steaks) · **Prep**: not stated · **Cook**: 24 h · **Rest**: not stated (seared immediately before serving)

**Ingredients**
- 2 pork shoulder steaks, **1 inch thick**
- 1 tsp kosher salt; ½ tsp black pepper
- 1 tsp vegetable oil (for searing)
- Optional purple cabbage slaw (cabbage, salt, pepper, lemon juice, Dijon, mayonnaise, olive oil)

**Method**
1. Sprinkle the steaks with salt and pepper; vacuum-seal each steak in its own bag (**circulator note**: bath device used — SousVide Supreme Demi in the pictures, Anova later).
2. **Circulator note**: cook in the water bath at **160 °F / 71 °C for 24 hours**.
3. Pat dry and sear in a preheated heavy pan (cast iron, 10 minutes preheat) **about 1 minute per side** — the source warns the steaks are "really tender, and may want to fall apart".
4. The source publishes no meat-core pull temperature; its only number is the **bath setting of 160 °F / 71 °C**, which by definition equals the final centre temperature after 24 hours. House guidance (not the source's): confirm with a probe — **Pull at 71 °C (160 °F)**. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source** (bath 160 °F / 71 °C).

### 2. Texas Monthly — Sous Vide Pork Steak
**Inspired by**: https://www.texasmonthly.com/recipe/sous-vide-pork-steak/ (fetched 2026-10-26). Texas Monthly (US, Texas barbecue journalism); individual recipe author not shown in the fetched page text. The page defines its cut explicitly: *"they're simply slices of pork from the Boston butt (pork shoulder)."*
**Serves**: not stated · **Prep**: not stated (+ 1 h rest per method trials) · **Cook**: 24–38 h in bag · **Rest**: 1 h (author's tested method; ice bath tried and rejected — the published procedure rests the bag)

**Ingredients**
- Pork shoulder ("pork steak") slices, preferably **1½–2 inches thick** (the author asks the butcher to cut thick from a 5–6 lb bone-in shoulder)
- Salt, applied generously
- Bold barbecue sauce (author used Braswell's Original), applied as a thin layer in the bag and again during browning

**Method**
1. Salt the steaks; brush a thin layer of barbecue sauce on the surface; bag and seal (**circulator note**: the author uses a Joule).
2. **Circulator note**: cook at **149 °F (65 °C)** — the source's tested and recommended pairing is *"a cooking temperature of 149 degrees for 36 hours"* (credit: Rob @QuestforBurgers), with the author's own trials run at **28 and 36 hours** and the stated finding that *"cooking times below 24 hours or above 48 hours weren't ideal for pork steak"*.
3. Rest the bag (the author rests every version after testing; an ice bath was tried and abandoned in favour of resting as-is).
4. Brown thoroughly on both sides in two steps, sauce, then brown again — surface dryness first so the Maillard reaction can start.
5. The source publishes no meat-core pull temperature; its numbers are **bath settings (149 °F) and bag times**. House guidance (not the source's): after 24+ hours at 149 °F the centre is at the bath — **Pull at 65 °C (149 °F)** by definition, then brown. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source** (bath 149 °F / 65 °C).

### 3. Thomas Gerlach kocht — Schweinenacken sous vide (Thomas Gerlach)
**Inspired by**: https://www.thomasgerlachkocht.de/schweinenacken-sous-vide/ (fetched 2026-10-26). Thomas Gerlach, *Thomas Gerlach kocht* (German).
**Serves**: not stated · **Prep**: not stated · **Cook**: ~4 h · **Rest**: optional ice-water chill for later use

**Ingredients**
- Schweinenacken (pork neck) in **2–3 cm thick slices**
- Salt, pepper; neutral vegetable oil
- Optional dry marinade: sugar, paprika, coriander, cumin, garlic powder, chili powder (curry offered in the article text)

**Method**
1. Rub with oil first if marinating so the spices adhere; apply the dry marinade or leave plain; vacuum-seal.
2. **Circulator note**: heat the water bath to **61 °C** — the source's verbatim reasoning: *"Wenn ich keine kleinen Kinder hätte, würde ich eventuell 60° wählen, was aber dann hygienisch schon die Untergrenze darstellt"* (without small children he might choose 60 °C, which is already the hygiene lower limit).
3. Cook **about four hours** — *"Eine halbe Stunde weniger oder eine oder zwei Stunden mehr spielen dabei keine Rolle"* (half an hour less or one to two hours more makes no difference).
4. Finish either by chilling in ice water for later use, or directly: grill very hot (**250–300 °C**) **1–2 minutes per side** for crust.
5. The source publishes no meat-core pull temperature; its **61 °C** is a bath setting, and it states plainly that pork must be cooked through for hygiene. House guidance (not the source's): the centre equals the bath — **Pull at 61 °C (142 °F)** before the grill finish. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source** (bath 61 °C).

## Temperature consensus

| Source | Tradition | Stated doneness evidence | Internal/pull temperature |
|---|---|---|---|
| DadCooksDinner (US) | American precision cooking | 24 h bath, sear 1 min/side | **No meat-core figure**; bath **160 °F / 71 °C** |
| Texas Monthly (US) | Texas barbecue, bag-and-brown | 28–36 h tested, recommended **149 °F / 36 h**; rest; two-step browning | **No meat-core figure**; bath **149 °F / 65 °C** |
| Thomas Gerlach (Germany) | German precision + grill finish | ~4 h bath, then 250–300 °C sear 1–2 min/side | **No meat-core figure**; bath **61 °C** (60 °C stated as hygiene floor) |

No source in this leaf publishes a pull temperature — all three numbers are **bath
settings**, and the leaf records them as such rather than as doneness readings (in
practice the centre equals the bath after these durations, which the house-guidance lines
note without presenting it as published). The observed bath spread is **61–71 °C
(142–160 °F)**, and it tracks two philosophies rather than one disagreement: the German
source wants 61 °C plus a hard sear for a nearly dissolving texture in 4 hours; the
American pair wants 65–71 °C held for a day or more so the steak slices. `recommended_doneness`
for this method: **no change proposed** — with no published pull temperature, the cut-level
`well_done` stands by default and this leaf contributes bath evidence only, recorded rather
than converted.

## What makes this method special

Sous vide is the only method in this cut's set with no gradient at all, which changes what
"done" means: instead of a surface racing ahead of a centre, the entire steak arrives at
one temperature simultaneously and then stays there while time — not heat — does the
tenderising. For a neck steak that matters because the cut's two desirable traits pull in
opposite directions under dry heat (fat wants high temperature to render; moisture wants
low temperature to keep), and the bath resolves the conflict by holding long enough for
collagen and fat to convert at a temperature the muscle fibres can survive. The three
sources' four-hour-versus-thirty-six-hour split is really a disagreement about which
outcome they want — melting tenderness with a crust added afterwards (DE), or a sliceable
steak that still holds a knife line (US) — and their 10 °C bath spread is the price of that
choice. What the method costs is everything the bath cannot do: no crust, no smoke, no
reduction, which is why every source ends with a hard, fast browning step outside the bag.

## Summary

Sous vide cooks a pork shoulder steak fully immersed at a fixed bath temperature — four
hours at 61 °C in Germany, 24–36 hours at 65–71 °C in the American pair — after which
every source browns the outside hard because the bath gives no crust of its own. It is the
only method here with no temperature gradient, which is why it suits a cut whose fat and
moisture want opposite heats, and why the sources disagree about time rather than about
doneness: the choice is melting-tender or sliceable. None of the three publishes a pull
temperature; their numbers are bath settings, recorded as such. If you use it, pick the
philosophy first, then sear as hot and dry as the surface will allow.
