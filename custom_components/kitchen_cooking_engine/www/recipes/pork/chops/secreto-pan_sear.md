# Secreto × Pan Sear — Recipe Temperature Research

## Cut profile

Pan searing — *a la plancha* in the cut's own tradition — is the method the Spanish sources
treat as the default for this cut: a thin, irregular sheet of heavily marbled shoulder-belly
muscle laid on a very hot plancha or grill plate for a few minutes per side, turned exactly
once, rested briefly, and salted **after** cooking. The five fetched sources agree on the
physics and disagree about the endpoint. The Spanish plancha recipes never mention an
internal temperature at all: Directo al Paladar judges the piece by its cut edge (*canto*) and
removes it "when it is to our liking, juicy and crunchy", while the Coren vermouth recipe
passes thin fillets over the plancha for barely 90 seconds per side. The Swiss-German source
is the only one that publishes a number, and it is deliberately low: **55–60 °C (131–140 °F)**
"rosa / medium rare", cooked 2–4 minutes per side in cast iron and finished with citrus
instead of salt. The German source cuts the meat into pieces, sears it in smoking-hot oil and
rests it in an 80 °C oven; the Swiss-French source sears 3–3½ minutes per side and rests five
minutes on a rack. The structural constant across all five: salt late (or not at all during
cooking), high heat briefly, one turn only, rest before slicing against the grain — the fat
seams of the *secreto* render in the first minute and a half, and every minute past that costs
juice rather than food safety.

## Styckesprofil

Planchastekning — *a la plancha* på snittets eget språk — är den metod de spanska källorna
behandlar som standard för detta stycke: ett tunt, oregelbundet blad av mycket marmorerad
skulder-buk-muskel på en mycket het plancha eller grillplatta ett par minuter per sida, vänd
exakt en gång, vilat kort och saltat **efter** stekningen. De fem hämtade källorna är eniga om
fysiken men oeniga om slutpunkten. De spanska plancharecepten nämner aldrig någon
innertemperatur: Directo al Paladar bedömer biten på dess kant (*canto*) och tar bort den "när
den är som vi vill ha den, saftig och krispig", medan Corens vermouthrecept vänder tunna fileer
i knappt 90 sekunder per sida. Den schweizisk-tyska källan är den enda som anger ett tal, och
det är medvetet lågt: **55–60 °C** "rosa / medium rare", stekt 2–4 minuter per sida i gjutjärn
och färdig med citrus i stället för salt. Den tyska källan skär köttet i bitar, bryner det i
rykande het olja och vilar det i 80 °C ugn; den schweizisk-franska bryner 3–3½ minuter per
sida och vilar fem minuter på galler. Den strukturella konstanten i alla fem: salta sent
(eller inte alls under tillagningen), hög värme kort, en vändning bara, vila före uppskärning
mot fiber — fettstrimlorna renderas i första minuten, och varje minut därefter kostar saft
snarare än livsmedelssäkerhet.

## Source recipes

### 1. Directo al Paladar — Receta de secreto de cerdo a la plancha con trucos para que te quede crujiente y jugoso (Pakus, Spain)
**Inspired by**: https://www.directoalpaladar.com/recetas-de-carnes-y-aves/receta-secreto-cerdo-a-plancha-trucos-te-quede-muy-crujiente-jugoso-interior (fetched 2026-10-26). Directo al Paladar (Spanish food media), editor Pakus, published 2024-12-05.
**Serves**: 2 · **Prep**: 4 min (plus 2 h tempering) · **Cook**: 8 min · **Rest**: 1–2 min

**Ingredients**
- 1 piece Ibérico pork secreto, about 600 g
- Salt and pepper, to taste
- Extra virgin olive oil, a few drops on the grill plate
- Vegetables for garnish, to taste, grilled

**Method**
1. Cut the secreto into **three or four pieces of uniform height** — the sheet is flat but irregular, with thick and thin zones, so uniform pieces cook evenly. Take the meat out of the fridge **two hours** before cooking so it reaches room temperature and does not shrink and deform on contact with the plancha.
2. Do **not** salt the meat. Preheat the plancha or grill plate very well. To improve contact, place a weight (a burger press) on the meat and cook **3–4 minutes on one side and 2–3 minutes on the other**; thinner pieces need only **2 minutes per side**.
3. Flip **once only**, with tongs — never a fork, which pierces the meat and loses juice. When turning, look at the **cut edge (*canto*)** to judge doneness.
4. When the meat is done to your liking, remove it from the plancha and **rest 1–2 minutes**. Then season with salt and pepper and serve with the chosen garnish.
5. The source publishes **no internal temperature**; doneness is judged by the cut edge and the cook's preference, and the article explicitly warns against overcooking. House guidance (not the source's): probe the thickest piece — **Pull at 63 °C (145 °F)**. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.

### 2. Chefkoch.de — Secreto vom Ibericoschwein, "Carco's schnelle Marinade" (user Carco, Germany)
**Inspired by**: Chefkoch.de community recipe "Secreto vom Ibericoschwein" (user Carco; bibliographic reference — Chefkoch.de recipe database, fetched 2026-04-27, record preserved in this leaf). German community-cooking tradition.
**Serves**: 2–3 · **Prep**: 15 min (plus marinating) · **Cook**: ~10 min · **Rest**: 5 min

**Ingredients**
- 500 g *secreto* of the Iberian pig
- Olive oil
- Salt and pepper
- **Carco's quick marinade**: oyster sauce, sweet soy sauce (*Kecap Manis*), olive oil — quantities to taste

**Method**
1. Wash the secreto and pat it dry, then cut it into pieces the width of one to two fingers. Marinate in oyster sauce, sweet soy sauce and olive oil.
2. Heat a pan to high temperature; the oil should be just short of smoking.
3. Sear the secreto for **3–4 minutes on the first side**, turn, and fry until done, turning as little as possible.
4. Before serving, let the meat **rest 5 minutes in an 80 °C oven** so the juices settle; season with salt and pepper to taste and serve with the marinade alongside.
5. The source publishes **no internal temperature**; the endpoint is the pan time and the sear. House guidance (not the source's): probe the thickest piece — **Pull at 63 °C (145 °F)**. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.

### 3. LUMA Delikatessen (Alex Bidault) — Secreto aux pommes de terre sautées et aux cèpes (Switzerland)
**Inspired by**: https://luma-delikatessen.ch/fr/magazin/recette/secreto-aux-pommes-de-terre-sautees-et-aux-cepes/ (fetched 2026-10-26). LUMA Delikatessen (Zürich meat house) recipe blog, recipe by caterer Alex Bidault (The French Kitchen Club, Zürich). Swiss-French tradition.
**Serves**: 2 · **Prep**: 20 min · **Cook**: 45 min total · **Rest**: 5 min (stated in method)

**Ingredients**
- About 400 g *secreto*
- A little kitchen salt
- A little Murray River salt
- A little freshly ground black pepper
- 1 handful ceps (porcini)
- 1 handful small potatoes
- 1 garlic clove
- A little butter
- A little olive oil
- 250 ml pork jus

**Method**
1. Let the meat thaw overnight in the refrigerator. Take it out one hour before preparation and let it come to room temperature.
2. Season the meat on both sides and **sear it in a hot pan 3–3½ minutes per side**. Let the meat **rest 5 minutes on a rack**. Do not rinse the pan.
3. Boil the potatoes until still firm. Halve the ceps and score the inner side. Halve the potatoes and sauté them in a pan with olive oil, a little butter and the pressed garlic clove.
4. Sear the ceps briefly in the same pan, outer side first, until golden; turn and cook 30 seconds more. Season with Murray River salt and pepper; cut into bite-sized pieces.
5. Reheat the pork jus, reduce it and mount the sauce with cold butter.
6. Slice the meat **against the grain**; plate the slices with the potatoes, ceps and pork jus.
7. The source publishes **no internal temperature**; the chef's note says only that this cut "supports high temperatures, but briefly only" and that the rest is very important. House guidance (not the source's): probe the thickest part — **Pull at 63 °C (145 °F)**. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.

### 4. Coren — Secreto de cerdo Selecta en salsa de vermouth (Spain)
**Inspired by**: https://www.coren.es/recetas/secreto-de-cerdo-selecta-en-salsa-de-vermouth/ (fetched 2026-10-26). Coren (Spanish meat cooperative, official recipes section). Spanish home-cooking tradition. **Quantity flag**: the page publishes its ingredient quantities in a block the fetch does not extract; the items below are exactly what the method names, with quantities marked as not published where the page gives none. **Tradition flag**: counted as the second Spanish source in this leaf — a duplication recorded here and deducted at cut level.
**Serves**: 4 (page states "cook in 30 minutes") · **Prep**: not stated · **Cook**: ~30 min total · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- *Secreto de cerdo* "Selecta", quantity not published (cut into thin fillets)
- Salt and pepper
- Onion and garlic, finely chopped, quantities not published
- Vermouth, quantity not published
- 1 glass of stock (*vaso de caldo*), quantity not published
- Flour, diluted in a little stock, quantity not published
- Olive oil

**Method**
1. Cut the *secreto* meat into thin fillets; season with salt and pepper and set aside.
2. In a frying pan, heat olive oil and gently fry the chopped onion and garlic.
3. When the onion is well softened (*pochada*), add the vermouth and the glass of stock and let it reduce; thicken everything with the flour diluted in a little stock.
4. On a very hot plancha or frying pan brushed with oil, pass the fillets **about 1½ minutes on each side**.
5. Plate the sauce first and centre the secreto on it, with a sprig of rosemary; the page suggests mashed potato or apple purée alongside.
6. The source publishes **no internal temperature**; the endpoint is 90 seconds per side over very high heat, and the thin fillets are cooked through by the sear. House guidance (not the source's): this thin form carries no probe in the source's tradition — **Pull at 63 °C (145 °F)**. Final serving temperature: **not stated by the source**.

### 5. meat4you — Rosa gebratenes Iberico Secreto mit Feigen-Chilisauce und Minze (Switzerland)
**Inspired by**: https://meat4you.ch/de/blog/cerdo-iberico-secreto-am-stuck (fetched 2026-10-26). meat4you (Swiss online butcher, Aargau), recipe blog. Swiss-German tradition. **Tradition flag**: counted as the second Swiss source in this leaf (with LUMA, source 3) — a duplication recorded here and deducted at cut level; it is retained because it is the only source in this leaf that publishes a core temperature.
**Serves**: 2 · **Prep**: 30 min tempering · **Cook**: 4–8 min · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 1 *secreto* (about 500 g)
- Salt and pepper, to taste
- 1 orange and 1 lemon
- 1 sprig thyme
- **Fig-chili sauce**: 200 g fresh figs; 150 ml dry white wine; 1 small red chili; 1–2 tbsp honey; 1–2 tbsp fresh mint, finely chopped; juice of ½ lemon; salt and pepper

**Method**
1. Let the Ibérico secreto rest at room temperature about **30 minutes** before searing, so it cooks evenly.
2. Heat a heavy pan (cast iron is best) over medium-high heat.
3. Lay the secreto in the pan with the thyme sprig and two thin lemon slices; do not crowd the pan. **Sear 2–4 minutes per side**, depending on thickness, until a golden-brown crust forms. (The source notes the same preparation works on the grill or a plancha.)
4. Check the core temperature: the source states the **ideal core temperature for Ibérico secreto is about 55–60 °C (131–140 °F) for medium rare**.
5. Do not salt during cooking. Slice against the grain and serve as a main or as tapas; at the table, press an orange and a lemon over the meat and put out a coarse fleur de sel.
6. Fig-chili sauce: stem and halve the figs; split, seed and finely chop the chili. Bring the white wine with the whole figs and chopped chili to a boil, reduce the heat and simmer **10–15 minutes** until the figs are soft and the sauce thickens. Stir in the honey and lemon juice; season with salt and pepper; fold in the mint just before serving.
7. **Pull at 55–60 °C (131–140 °F)** (stated — the source gives a core-temperature window rather than a pull point). Final serving temperature: **55–60 °C / 131–140 °F** (stated, "medium rare / rosa").

### 6. Madensverden — Pandestegning af koteletter (Denmark) — NOT COUNTED (different cut; technique reference only)
**Inspired by**: Madensverden "pandestegning" technique (Danish home-cooking tradition, fetched 2026-04-27, record preserved in this leaf). **Not counted toward this leaf's source gate**: the source is a pork-chop (*svinekotelet*) pan-frying technique, not a *secreto* recipe — a generalisation, not a cut-specific source. It is kept as the Danish *pandestegning* reference for how the pan technique treats a marbled chop.
**Serves**: 2 · **Prep**: 5 min · **Cook**: ~8 min · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 2 pork chops (*koteletter*)
- Butter and a little oil
- Salt and pepper

**Method**
1. Pat the chops dry and season them. Heat butter and oil in a pan until the fat foams.
2. Fry the chops over medium-high heat until well coloured, turn once, and cook through.
3. Danish practice for a high-fat collar cut such as *secreto* finishes it around 68–70 °C so the fat seams render — a deliberate contrast with the rosa cluster in the counted sources, recorded here as technique context and not as a *secreto* source's doneness data. Final serving temperature: **not applicable — not a counted source**.

One of the five counted sources publishes a number, and it is deliberately the lowest reading
in this method's corpus: **55–60 °C (131–140 °F)**, the Swiss butcher's "rosa" window. The
other four publish no temperature whatsoever and judge by time (90 seconds to 4 minutes per
side), by the cut edge, and by a crust — so **no numerical range can honestly be derived from
them**; the house-guidance 63 °C pull lines in sources 1–4 are what would carry the meat to
the USDA whole-muscle floor, and they are marked as house guidance, not as source data. The
real divergence this leaf records is cultural: the German-language butcher cluster (this
source at 55–60 °C, corroborated by uncounted German blog leads at 56–62 °C) cooks Ibérico
secreto *rosa*, while Spanish home cooking — including the uncounted Danish reference's
finishing practice for high-fat collar cuts — takes a well-marbled piece further so the seams
render. Both schools agree on the process constants: no salt during cooking (or salt after),
one flip, rest, slice against the grain. `recommended_doneness` for this method: **no change
proposed** — the only measured source sits below the cut's `medium` band (63 °C), but one
counted source is not enough to open a `medium_rare` band at cut level; the 55–62 °C cluster
is recorded here as divergence for future work.

## What makes this method special

The plancha is the only method that matches the *secreto*'s geometry: a thin, wide, irregular
sheet with coarse fat seams gets maximum contact heat across its whole surface, renders the
seams in the first ninety seconds, and is finished before the interior can dry — which is
exactly why the Spanish sources refuse to give it to the oven or the pot and reserve those
techniques for thicker cuts like *presa*. Two details do most of the work. First, the late
salt: three of the counted sources salt after cooking (or not at all before it) because early
salt draws surface juice onto a piece that has no thickness to spare. Second, the single turn
with tongs: Directo al Paladar's "look at the *canto*" is the whole doneness method — on a
1–2 cm sheet the cut edge shows the gradient that a probe would measure, and turning once
keeps the crust intact. The Swiss-German source pushes the same geometry the other way: cast
iron, citrus instead of salt, and a deliberately rosa core at 55–60 °C, leaning on the fat
marbling of the Ibérico animal (whose oleic-acid fat is safe to eat at lower whole-muscle
doneness in its own tradition) rather than on the well-done convention. The rest is
non-negotiable in every source: 1–5 minutes on a rack or in a warm oven, so the muscle
reabsorbs what the sear pushed out.

## Summary

Pan searing is the home method for *secreto*: very hot plancha, one turn, minutes per side,
salt after, rest, slice against the grain. Five counted sources describe that process almost
identically and then part ways on the endpoint — one Swiss butcher pulls at 55–60 °C rosa,
the Spanish and German sources publish no temperature at all, and the Spanish tradition leans
well done so the fat seams render. Because the cut is a thin marbled sheet, the window between
crusty-and-juicy and dry is about two minutes, so the cut edge and a short rest matter more
than any single target number. The 55–62 °C German-language cluster is the open question this
leaf records for future doneness work.
