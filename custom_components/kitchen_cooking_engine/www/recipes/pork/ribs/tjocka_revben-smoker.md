## Cut profile

Smoking is the method most often named when tjocka revben is cooked outside Sweden, and the method
where the cut's own BBQ tradition lives: the cracked, meaty front racks take a long stay in thin blue
smoke far better than the thin Swedish retail ribs do, because the thick belly layer carries smoke
flavour deep and renders slowly at pit temperature. The three fetched sources describe cooking low and slow —
107–130 °C — with hardwood (apple, hickory, oak), and all three treat the smoke as the primary
seasoning, with rub and (optionally) sauce layered around it. The cut's uneven thickness is managed
by placement relative to the fire rather than by turning: the Swedish source positions the rack over
a drip pan with a deflector, the Australian source turns once during the first hour, and the American
source refuses to flip at all. Probe placement: from the side into the thickest belly-side meat, away
from bone and away from the cracked bone ends; note the American source's warning that on ribs the
bones distort probe readings and the bend test is the pitmaster's real instrument. Carryover is
small-to-moderate at these endpoints; the Swedish source rests 30 minutes, the American source rests
after saucing.

## Styckesprofil

Rökning är metoden där tjocka revben har sin BBQ-tradition: de knäckta, köttrika framre spjälten tål
länge i tunn blå rök betre än de tynna svenska detaljhandelsspjälten, eftersom det tjocka köttlagret
bär röksmaken djupt och renderar långsamt vid gropstemperatur. Samtliga tre hämtade källor arbetar
lågt och långsamt — 107–130 °C — med fruktträ (äpple, hickory, ek). Ojämna tjockleken hanteras genom

## Source recipes

### 1. Swedish (Grilltips.se, Conny Arvidsson) — Barbecue grillade tjocka revben
**Inspired by**: Conny Arvidsson, Grilltips.se; https://grilltips.se/grillrecept/kott/tjocka-revben (fetched 2026-10-31). *Written for this exact cut ("Tjocka revbensspjäll"), kamado-style indirect smoking with apple or hickory, butcher-paper wrap at 80–85 °C. Published 2017-11-28, updated 2025-11-04.*
**Serves**: 4–6 · **Prep**: 30 min · **Cook**: 6 h · **Rest**: 30 min

**Ingredients**
- Tjocka revben (Swedish pork), racks
- BBQ-rub (e.g. Grill & BBQ Rub)
- **Moppsås**: 1 dl äppelcidervinäger (apple cider vinegar), 2 dl äppeljuice (apple juice), 0,5 msk worcestershiresås, 1 msk rub
- Droppformar (drip pans), slaktarpapper (butcher paper)

**Method**
1. Trim excess fat but leave some for juiciness; season with your favourite rub and refrigerate until grilling.
2. Install the heat deflector and drip pans; light the coals and **stabilise the grill at ca 120–130 °C**.
3. Add smoke chunks (apple or hickory); wait until the smoke turns thin and blue-grey ("clean").
4. Place the ribs over the drip pan and leave undisturbed **at least 1 hour**; check that heat and smoke stay even.
5. After 2–3 hours, mop with the mop sauce if the surface looks dry. Insert the MEATER probe from the side into the thickest belly-side meat, away from bone and cracked bone ends.
6. **When the meat reaches 80–85 °C, wrap in butcher paper** (or foil) and continue **to 90–95 °C or until a probe slides in tender**.
7. Rest **at least 30 minutes**; slice between the bones and serve with BBQ sauce.
8. **Pull at 90–95 °C** (194–203 °F) — stated by the source. The same page carries a real public disagreement worth preserving: the author states in the comments that he pulls at **90–93 °C** to melt fat and sinew ("tjocka revben är fett kött"), while a commenter insists the ribs should come off at **70 °C** with the grill at max 125 °C; the page FAQ answers *"Tjocka revben är egentligen klara redan vid 75 °C, men det är först vid 90 °C som fettet smälter"* (ready at 75 °C, but fat only melts at 90 °C).
9. Final serving temperature: **not stated by source** — rest 30 minutes covered before slicing.

### 2. Australian (Gourmet Traveller, Papi Chulo, Manly NSW) — Smoked pork ribs with barbecue sauce
**Inspired by**: Papi Chulo (restaurant, Manly NSW), Australian Gourmet Traveller; https://www.gourmettraveller.com.au/recipe/chefs-recipes/smoked-pork-ribs-with-barbecue-sauce-and-vietnamese-style-coleslaw-9168/ (fetched 2026-10-31). *Chef-attributed restaurant recipe; "American-style pork rib racks"; applewood and oak; low coal barbecue at 120 °C.*
**Serves**: 6–8 · **Prep**: 45 min · **Cook**: 3 h 20 min · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 4 American-style pork rib racks (about 700 gm each)
- For smoking: applewood and oak chips, soaked 1 hour in cold water, drained
- **Barbecue rub**: 80 gm sea salt flakes, 2½ tbsp smoked paprika, 2 tbsp black peppercorns coarsely crushed, 1 tbsp onion powder, 2 tsp garlic powder, ½ tsp each smoked paprika and cayenne
- **Barbecue sauce**: 2½ tbsp tomato sauce, 2 tbsp brown sugar, 1 tbsp soy sauce, 15 ml hot sauce, 2 tsp black peppercorns coarsely crushed, 1 tsp each onion powder, garlic powder and Korean chilli powder, ½ tsp each smoked paprika and sweet paprika
- Vietnamese-style coleslaw, to serve

**Method**
1. Preheat a coal barbecue to low heat (**120 °C**). Combine the rub ingredients and rub all over the ribs; set aside.
2. Add the soaked wood chips to the hot coals and heat until smoking; grill the ribs **turning once until starting to colour (1 hour)**.
3. **Wrap in foil** and cook, topping up coal and wood chips as necessary, **until tender and the meat falls off the bone (1½–2 hours)**. Insert the MEATER probe from the side into the thickest rack, away from bone, once wrapped.
4. Meanwhile combine the barbecue sauce ingredients with 2 tsp water and let the flavours develop 30–35 minutes.
5. **Pull temperature: not stated by source.** The source describes tender meat falling off the bone after 2½–3 hours at 120 °C.
6. Final serving temperature: **not stated by source** — unwrap, brush thickly with the sauce mixture and serve hot with the Vietnamese-style coleslaw.

### 3. American (Meathead's AmazingRibs.com) — Meathead's Last Meal Smoked Pork Ribs
**Inspired by**: Meathead Goldwyn, AmazingRibs.com; https://amazingribs.com/best-barbecue-ribs-recipe/ (fetched 2026-10-31). *"The #1 best barbecue ribs recipe", for St. Louis cut, spare ribs or baby back; classic Southern method at 107 °C with the bend test as doneness instrument. Rated 4.76/5, 1601 votes.*
**Serves**: 3 (1 slab) · **Prep**: 30 min + dry brine · **Cook**: 5–7 h · **Rest**: after saucing

**Ingredients**
- 1 slab ribs — any cut (baby back, spare ribs, St. Louis cut)
- 4 tablespoons Meathead's Memphis Dust rub
- 1 teaspoon Morton coarse kosher salt (¼ teaspoon per pound of meat — ribs are about 50 % bone)
- 6 tablespoons barbecue sauce (optional; Memphis-style dry serving is authentic)

**Method**
1. Rinse the ribs in cool water to remove bone bits. Remove the membrane from the bone side (butter knife under it, grip with a paper towel, peel) or slash it every inch. Trim excess fat from both sides.
2. Dry brine: sprinkle the kosher salt (¼ tsp per pound of ribs) and give it **1–2 hours** to be absorbed. Before adding a commercial rub, check for double salt — most rubs contain salt.
3. Coat the meat with a thin layer of water (or mustard as glue) and apply the rub generously.
4. Fire up the smoker or set up the grill for **2-zone (indirect) cooking at about 225 °F (107 °C)**; do not go under 200 °F (93 °C) or over 250 °F (121 °C). Add about 4 oz (113 g) of dry wood near the flame.
5. Put the slabs in over indirect heat, **meaty side up**; close the lid. When the smoke dwindles after 20–30 minutes add another 4 oz of wood — then **no more wood** (over-smoking ruins the meal). Do not flip; rotate slabs position halfway if more than one is on.
6. The Texas Crutch (foil with ~1 oz water) is optional and capped at **1 hour** in this source's view; the popular 3-2-1 method's 2 hours in foil is explicitly rejected as mush-making.
7. Cook **5–7 hours for St. Louis cut or spare ribs** (3–5 for baby back). Doneness by the **bend test**: pick up the slab with tongs and bounce gently — if the surface cracks, it is ready. The source's own thermometer guidance: on ribs the bones distort readings and the meat is thin; if you do probe with a very thin probe away from bone, **shoot for about 200 °F (93 °C)**.
8. Sauce: paint both sides with barbecue sauce and cook **15 minutes** more — no earlier, the sugar burns. Optional: sizzle the sauced slab briefly over the hottest zone to crisp the sauce.
9. **Pull at 93 °C** (200 °F) — the source's stated probe target, reached via bend test rather than by continuous probing.
10. Final serving temperature: **not stated by source** — slice between the bones; Memphis-style dry ribs are served without sauce.

### 4. Indian (Usha's Kitchen, Usha Martin) — Indian spiced ribs, 2:2:1 method
**Inspired by**: Usha's Kitchen; https://ushaskitchen.com/recipe/indian-spiced-baby-back-ribs/ (read 2026-11-01). *An Indian-spiced dry rub and tamarind barbecue sauce cooked by the 2:2:1 method; the source states a 190 °F internal target, which is high for baby backs and matches a thick, meaty rack rather than a lean one.*
**Serves**: 6 · **Prep**: 20 min · **Cook**: 5 h · **Rest**: yes, unspecified

**Ingredients**
- 6 lb baby back pork ribs
- 4 tbsp Curry Starter (*the author's spice paste*); 1 tbsp olive oil, as binder
- 2 tbsp Keema Spice Blend, for the rub; olive oil spray
- **Sauce**: 2 tbsp Keema Spice Blend; ¼ cup water; 2 tbsp tomato paste; 1 tbsp tamarind concentrate; 1 tbsp butter; ¼ cup dark brown sugar; ¾ cup water

**Method**
1. Blend the Curry Starter with the olive oil to a smooth binder; set half aside for the sauce.
2. Build the sauce: fry the remaining starter with the spice blend, add ¼ cup water and simmer until it separates, then the tomato paste, tamarind, butter, sugar and ¾ cup water; simmer to thickness.
3. Apply the binder, then a light coat of the rub.
4. Cook at **250 °F** by 2:2:1 — 2 hours uncovered over indirect heat with a moisture pan, 2 hours wrapped in foil, 1 hour at **225 °F** brushed with the sauce.
5. Insert the MEATER probe from the side into the thickest part of the rack, away from bone, once the rub has set.
6. **Pull at 190 °F (88 °C)** — the source's stated internal temperature, with the meat pulling easily from the bone.
7. Final serving temperature: **not stated by source** — rest before slicing.

### 5. Levantine-spiced (Hark BBQ, Alana Alderson) — Middle Eastern pork ribs with pomegranate BBQ sauce
**Inspired by**: Hark BBQ; https://harkbbq.com/blogs/recipes/middle-eastern-pork-ribs-with-pomegranate-bbq-sauce (read 2026-11-01). *Aleppo pepper, sumac and pomegranate molasses on an offset smoker; the only Group C source found that states two internal checkpoints.*
**Serves**: 4 · **Prep**: 20 min · **Cook**: 4–6 h · **Rest**: a few minutes

**Ingredients**
- 1 rack pork ribs
- **Rub**: 2 tbsp brown sugar; 1½ tbsp each ground coriander, ground cumin and Aleppo pepper; 1½ tsp kosher salt; ½ tsp each ground cinnamon, ground fennel and black pepper
- **Sauce**: 90 g tomato paste; 3 tbsp blackstrap molasses; 2 tsp paprika; 2 tbsp olive oil; 2 sprigs thyme; ½ cup pomegranate molasses; ¼ cup brown sugar; 1 tbsp each garlic and onion powder; 1 tbsp sumac; 1 tbsp sea salt; 1 bay leaf; 1 cinnamon stick; ½ cup pomegranate juice; ½ tbsp Aleppo pepper
- Butter and honey, for the wrap

**Method**
1. Bring an offset smoker to a steady **250 °F (121 °C)**.
2. Remove the membrane from the bone side, brush with olive oil and apply the rub; rest 15–20 minutes until the surface looks wet.
3. Smoke bone side down **3–4 hours**, until the internal temperature is about **165 °F (74 °C)**.
4. Wrap in two sheets of foil with butter and honey; return to the smoker **1–2 hours**, until **195–205 °F (90–96 °C)**.
5. Probe from the side into the thickest meat, away from bone, at each checkpoint; the source gives both numbers explicitly.
6. **Pull at 195–205 °F (90–96 °C)** — the source's stated finishing window.
7. Final serving temperature: **not stated by source** — rest briefly, slice between the bones, glaze every 10 minutes over the last 30 minutes.

## What makes this method special

Smoke is a surface treatment applied by a gas: hardwood combustion deposits carbonyls and phenols on the cold, damp meat while the surface is still wet enough to hold them, and only later does the crust those compounds form become the bark that seals the exterior. This cut is unusually good at absorbing that treatment, because the thick meat cover gives the smoke somewhere to sit instead of the thin, dry surface a leaner rack presents, and its fat renders slowly enough at pit heat to baste the meat from inside while the bark builds outside. The second feature is the cut's uneven thickness, which all three of the fetched European, American and Asian descriptions solve the same way — by placement relative to the fire rather than by turning — since a thick front rack moved constantly cooks at the thin end first. Wrapping is the one genuinely contested decision: it speeds the cook and softens the bark, and the sources disagree openly about how much of it is tolerable. Carryover at these endpoints is small to moderate, but the bones themselves distort probe readings, which is why every source that can uses a mechanical test alongside the thermometer.

## Summary

Smoking cooks these ribs low and slow over hardwood until the connective tissue in the thick front meat has turned to gelatin and the outside has set into a dark, spiced bark. It is the method that suits this cut best of any in the tree: the meat cover holds smoke and renders its own fat through the long cook, and the uneven thickness is handled by where you put the rack, not by fussing with it. The cost is time and attention — a whole afternoon at pit heat, with the wrap decision made halfway — and the reward is a flavour no indoor method reproduces.
