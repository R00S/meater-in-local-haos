# Cured Ham × Smoker — Recipe Temperature Research

## Cut profile

Smoking a cured ham is the corpus's clearest example of a method whose name promises more than it
delivers: the ham is **already cured and already smoked**, so "double smoked" means adding a
second, thinner layer of wood smoke while reheating it. The cut is the pig's **hind leg** — cured
around the femur, rind on, with a fat layer under the skin — and in the spiral-sliced form that
dominates this leaf, the leg has been cut into a continuous spiral so the smoke and glaze can
reach every slice. That cut is what the method exploits: the slices fan open, smoke deposits on
surfaces that would otherwise be interior, and the glaze runs down between them. Carryover is
small and the sources treat it as such: two pull at 140 °F and one calls 120 °F its finish, and
none of them is chasing a doneness — they are chasing a surface.

## Source recipes

### 1. Meat Church (Matt Pittman, US) — Glazed Double Smoked Ham with Apple Cherry Habanero Bourbon Glaze
**Inspired by**: https://www.meatchurch.com/blogs/recipes/double-smoked-ham (fetched 2026-10-26, published 2020-04-08). Meat Church (US barbecue company). US Texas barbecue tradition — **Culinary Group B** source.
**Serves**: not stated (a 4.5 kg spiral ham) · **Prep**: 10 min · **Cook**: ~3½ h · **Rest**: 15 min

**Ingredients**
- 1 spiral-sliced fully cooked ham, about 4.5 kg (10 lb)
- Meat Church Honey Bacon BBQ rub, applied liberally
- **Glaze**: 1⅓ cups light brown sugar; 4 tbsp cherry preserves; 60 ml (2 oz) apple-cherry habanero pepper jelly; 60 ml (2 oz) clover honey; 60 ml (2 oz) bourbon
- Olive oil or duck-fat spray, as a binder

**Method**
1. Set the smoker to **250 °F (121 °C)** with a medium wood such as hickory or pecan (the source used post oak in its video).
2. Rinse and pat the ham dry, wipe it with oil as a binder, and season liberally with the rub.
3. Put the ham on a rack over a disposable pan to catch the glaze, and place it in the smoker.
4. **The target is 140 °F (60 °C) internal.** Monitor with a leave-in probe in the deepest part, or check periodically with an instant-read.
5. Make the glaze when the ham reaches **100 °F (38 °C)** — the source's timing, "because we will start basting at 110 and repeat that every 10–15 degrees". Simmer the preserves, sugar, pepper jelly and honey, add the bourbon and reduce by a quarter.
6. Start basting at **110 °F (43 °C)** and repeat every 10–15 degrees of rise.
7. **Pull at 60 °C (140 °F)** — the source's own figure, and the reason for its basting schedule: the glaze is built up in layers as the ham heats, so the last baste goes on just before the target is reached.
8. Rest **15 minutes**, then carve. Final serving temperature: **not stated beyond the 140 °F**. The source notes an 11 lb ham took **3½ hours** at 250 °F.

### 2. Hey Grill Hey (Susie Bulloch, US) — Twice Smoked Ham with Brown Sugar Honey Glaze
**Inspired by**: https://heygrillhey.com/twice-smoked-ham/ (fetched 2026-10-26, published 2020-04-10). Hey Grill Hey (US barbecue site), 47 reviews. US backyard-smoker tradition — **Culinary Group B** source. **Record flag**: the page's printable card did not render in the fetched text, so this record is built from the article body plus the ingredient list; the step order is the source's four-stage description ("smoke, braise, glaze, broil"), with quantities from its stated list.
**Serves**: not stated (a 3.6 kg ham) · **Prep**: 10 min · **Cook**: ~2 h · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 1 spiral-sliced ham, 3.6 kg (8 lb), pre-smoked
- 1 cup chicken stock (for the braise)
- **Glaze**: 1 cup brown sugar; ¼ cup honey; 1 tbsp Dijon mustard; 2 tsp Worcestershire sauce; 1 tsp ground cinnamon; 1 tsp ground ginger; ⅛ tsp ground cloves
- 1 tbsp Signature Sweet Rub

**Method**
1. **Smoke**: put the ham on the smoker at low heat with the surface exposed so the smoke can work on the meat.
2. **Braise**: add the chicken stock and cover tightly to keep in as much moisture as possible.
3. **Glaze**: brush the brown sugar and honey glaze over the ham.
4. **Broil**: finish under high heat (the article moves the ham to a cast-iron skillet and then the oven's broiler) to crisp and caramelise the glaze.
5. On temperature, the source's own words: below **140 °F** smaller hams get there in less time — "smaller hams will reach 140 degrees F in less time and larger hams will take longer. A 10–12 pound ham will need 2.5–3 hours in the braising step." The FAQ also states the smoker is raised to **300 °F** for the final two hours.
6. **Pull at 60 °C (140 °F)** — the source's own figure, quoted from its FAQ rather than its card.
7. Final serving temperature: **not stated**; the ham goes from braise to glaze to broil and is served hot.

### 3. Smoking-Meat.com (Jeff Phillips, US) — Double Smoked Holiday Ham Glazed with Maple Syrup
**Inspired by**: https://www.smoking-meat.com/double-smoked-holiday-ham-glazed-with-maple-syrup (fetched 2026-10-26). Smoking-Meat.com (US barbecue site), 4.5/5 from 36 votes. US barbecue tradition — **Culinary Group B** source. **The outlier**: this is the only record in the corpus that names a finish temperature *below* the others.
**Serves**: not stated (a 4.5 kg spiral ham) · **Prep**: 10 min · **Cook**: 3 h · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 1 spiral-sliced ham, about 4.5 kg (10 lb)
- 2 cups real maple syrup
- ¼ cup barbecue rub (the source's own "original rub")
- 10 toothpicks
- Pecan wood, for smoke

**Method**
1. Sit the ham in a shallow pan. Peel back each slice and douse it with maple syrup, then apply the rub to every slice, plus the front, sides and back.
2. Pin the front slice in place with toothpicks so it does not fall over during the cook.
3. Set the smoker for **indirect cooking at 225 °F (107 °C)**, with a water pan if the smoker has one, and put the pan of ham on the grate.
4. Smoke **3–4 hours**, glazing the outside about **once an hour** with a mix of ½ cup maple syrup and 2 tbsp rub (or with the syrup-and-rub drippings in the pan).
5. The page's own framing: *"remember you are not cooking the ham, only warming it through and letting the smoke kiss the ham for a few hours."*
6. **Pull at 49 °C (120 °F)** — the source's stated "Meat Finish Temp" of **120 °F**, the lowest figure in this cut's corpus, and defensible only because the ham is fully cooked before it is smoked (see the analysis below).
7. Final serving temperature: **not stated**; the source's endpoint is the crispiness of the edges — "when the ham starts to get some nice crispiness to the edges … call it done".

## Temperature consensus

| Source | Tradition | Smoker setting | Published finish temperature |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meat Church (US) | Texas barbecue, 250 °F | **250 °F (121 °C)**, ~3½ h | **140 °F (60 °C)**, basting from 110 °F |
| Hey Grill Hey (US) | US backyard smoker, braise + broil | low → **300 °F** for the last 2 h | **140 °F (60 °C)** |
| Smoking-Meat (US) | US barbecue, 225 °F | **225 °F (107 °C)**, 3–4 h | **120 °F (49 °C)** |

Two sources say **140 °F** and one says **120 °F**, and the disagreement is instructive rather
than sloppy. 140 °F is the conventional reheating target for a cured ham and the figure the
oven-roast leaf reaches independently; 120 °F is what a pitmaster pulls at when the ham is
already fully cooked, the smoke has been applied for three hours, and the endpoint being judged
is the **crispiness of the outer slices** rather than the safety of the centre. For a home cook
using a supermarket spiral ham, 140 °F is the defensible target and matches the engine; 120 °F is
recorded here as the corpus's low end with the reasoning the source gives. `recommended_doneness`
for this method: **`heated_through`** (60 °C), consistent with the cut and with two of the three
records.

## What makes this method special

Three mechanics separate a smoked ham from an oven one. First, **the smoke reaches interior
surfaces that an oven cannot**: a spiral cut fans open, and every source here works glaze or rub
down between the slices — Meat Church bastes as the ham climbs, Smoking-Meat douses each slice
with syrup before the cook starts. That is a surface-area play, not a temperature play. Second,
**the cook is a reheat, and the sources know it**: Smoking-Meat says so in as many words, and
Meat Church's basting schedule (start at 110 °F, repeat every 10–15 degrees) only makes sense if
the ham is already safe and the pitmaster is decorating it. Third, **the finish is a glaze
problem**: Hey Grill Hey's four-stage sequence — smoke, braise, glaze, broil — is the most
explicit statement of that in the corpus, and it introduces the one step no smoker can do on its
own, a high-heat broil to set the sugar. The braise stage is the other distinctive move: covering
the ham with stock keeps it from drying during the hours of smoke, which is the same logic as the
oven leaf's foil, arrived at from the other direction.

## Summary

Double-smoking a cured ham is three or four hours at 225–250 °F to an internal 140 °F, with the
glaze applied in layers as the ham climbs rather than all at once. Work the glaze or rub down
between the spiral slices, keep a water pan or a foil cover on to stop it drying, and finish under
high heat so the sugar sets. One respected source pulls at 120 °F because the ham is already
cooked; 140 °F is the safer and more conventional target. Nothing here is about doneness — it is
about smoke on surfaces and a crust that does not burn.
