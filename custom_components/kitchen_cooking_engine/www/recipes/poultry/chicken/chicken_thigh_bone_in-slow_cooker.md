## Cut profile

Slow-cooker braises (dakjjim-style) exploit the bone-in thigh's tolerance for long, gentle
moist heat: the appliance holds roughly 85–95 °C internally on low or high settings, so the
thigh's collagen converts to gelatin over 4–6 hours while the muscle never approaches the
temperatures at which breast meat dries out. The bone-in, skin-on format is explicitly
recommended by the fetched Korean source because the skin and bone keep the pieces intact
through the long cook — though the skin turns soft and rubbery rather than crisp, and many
cooks trim or brown it first. Vegetables (potato, carrot, onion, mushrooms) cook alongside
the chicken in a soy-sweet braising sauce.

This leaf records one fetched Korean source; slow-cooker bone-in thigh recipes exist in
other traditions (e.g. Caribbean brown stew), but no second qualifying recipe was fetched
this session, so this is partial research.

## Source recipes

### 1. Korean Bapsang — Slow Cooker Dakjjim (Korean Braised Chicken)
**Inspired by**: https://www.koreanbapsang.com/slow-cooker-dakjjim-korean-braised/ (fetched 2026-10-24). Hyosun Ro, Korean Bapsang; Korean tradition (dakjjim). The page states that when buying cut-up chicken, "bone-in and skin-on thighs and drum sticks will be the best for this recipe."

**Serves**: 4 · **Prep**: not stated · **Cook**: 4 h on high or 6 h on low (+ optional 6–8 min reduction) · **Rest**: not stated

**Ingredients**
- 1 medium-size chicken, cut up (about 2.5 pounds of cut pieces; bone-in, skin-on thighs and drumsticks recommended)
- 2 medium potatoes, cut into large chunks
- 1 medium carrot, cut into large chunks
- 1/2 large onion, cut into large chunks
- 4–5 mushroom caps (shiitake, white, or baby bella), cut into large chunks
- 6–8 plump garlic cloves
- 3–4 thin ginger slices (1-inch round)
- 2–3 dried whole red chili peppers (or green chili peppers / jalapeños, optional)
- 2 scallions, roughly chopped

*Sauce*
- 1/3 cup soy sauce
- 2 tablespoons dark brown sugar
- 2 tablespoons oyster sauce (or 2 more tablespoons soy sauce)
- 3 tablespoons rice wine or mirin
- 2 tablespoons honey (or 2–3 more tablespoons brown sugar)
- 1/4 teaspoon pepper
- 2 teaspoons sesame oil
- 1 teaspoon sesame seeds

**Method**
1. Clean and cut the chicken into 2–3-inch pieces. Trim excess fat; drain thoroughly or pat dry with paper towels.
2. Put the chicken and vegetables (except the scallions) in the slow cooker. Pour in the sauce and toss everything well. The page notes this can be prepared ahead and refrigerated at this point.
3. Cover and cook about **4 hours on high or 6 hours on low**. If home, stir midway to rotate the chicken.
4. With a few minutes remaining, stir in the scallions, sesame oil, and sesame seeds; alternatively follow the optional reduction step.
5. **Optional**: transfer everything to a large pot and cook uncovered over high stovetop heat until the liquid is reduced and slightly thickened, **6–8 minutes**; stir in the scallions, sesame oil and sesame seeds and turn off the heat once the scallions are slightly wilted.

**Probe and endpoint**: the source gives no probe instruction. **no internal temperature stated** — only the appliance settings (high/low) and time.

## What makes this method special

The slow cooker holds the thigh in a sealed moist-heat environment near simmering temperature for hours, so the result is controlled collagen conversion with essentially no browning unless the meat is seared first. The bone-in, skin-on piece survives the long cook intact where boneless pieces would shred, and the surrounding vegetables absorb rendered fat and soy-sweet braising liquid. The trade-off is entirely textural: no crust, soft skin, and a doneness endpoint defined by time rather than temperature.

## Summary

Slow cooking turns bone-in thighs into spoon-tender braised meat in a soy-sweet sauce, using the cut's collagen as an asset over a 4–6 hour gentle simmer. It is an excellent match for the cut's forgiving nature, but it sacrifices all crust and gives only time-based doneness. One Korean source supports the method; temperature claims must wait for sources that measure.
