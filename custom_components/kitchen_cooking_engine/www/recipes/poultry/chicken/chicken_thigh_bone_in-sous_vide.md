## Cut profile

Sous vide is the only method where the cooking temperature *is* the serving temperature:
bone-in, skin-on thighs sealed in a bag reach the bath's temperature and stay there, so the
cook chooses doneness directly — 66 °C (150 °F) gives firm, juicy meat, 74 °C (165 °F)
gives tender juicy meat, and holding 74 °C for 4–8 hours converts the collagen into
fall-off-the-bone texture. The bone-in format is explicitly recommended by the fetched
source; the bone may be removed after cooking. Because the bag prevents any Maillard
reaction, the skin is finished separately in a hot skillet — the classic two-stage
sous-vide-plus-sear structure. Pasteurisation is achieved by time at temperature rather
than by a high endpoint, which is why bath charts substitute for pull temperatures here.

This leaf records one fetched source (Anova / J. Kenji López-Alt). Sous-vide thigh recipes
from other traditions were not fetched this session, so this is partial research.

## Source recipes

### 1. J. Kenji López-Alt / Anova Culinary Recipes — Chicken Thigh
**Inspired by**: https://recipes.anovaculinary.com/recipe/chicken-thigh (fetched 2026-10-24). J. Kenji López-Alt, Anova Culinary Recipes; US tradition. The page explicitly says "bone-in and skin-on recommended" when cooking thighs on their own.

**Serves**: labelled "Ingredients for 1" · **Prep**: 5 min · **Cook**: 1 h (recipe setting; chart times 1–8 h) · **Rest**: no fixed rest; ice-bath chill described

**Ingredients**
- Chicken thigh (bone-in and skin-on recommended)
- Salt
- Pepper
- Aromatics: thyme, garlic, rosemary (optional)
- 1 tablespoon / 15 ml vegetable, canola or rice bran oil, for finishing

*Optional pan sauce (listed on the page)*
- Minced shallots or garlic
- A glass of wine or a shot of liquor
- A pat of butter
- Lemon juice

**Method**
1. Preheat the precision cooker and bring the water bath to the desired temperature; the page refers to its timing chart for choosing temperature and time.
2. Generously season bone-in, skin-on chicken thighs (drumsticks or legs may be used too) with salt and pepper.
3. Fold the top of a vacuum-seal or zipper-lock bag over to form a hem. Add the optional aromatics. Seal with a vacuum sealer or use the water-displacement method for a zipper-lock bag.
4. Put the bag in the bath without blocking the cooker's intake or output. Cook for the time corresponding to the chosen bath temperature.
5. Transfer the cooked bag to an ice bath and chill completely; the page says the chicken can be refrigerated up to three days before finishing and serving.
6. Remove the chicken from the bag, discard the aromatics if used, and pat the chicken dry. Reserve the gelled bag juices if making a pan sauce.
7. Heat the finishing oil in a non-stick or cast-iron skillet over medium heat until shimmering. Add the chicken skin-side down and press slightly for good contact. Cook until the skin is very brown and crisp, **4–5 minutes total**.
8. Flip and cook the second side just until warmed through, about **1 minute**. Transfer to a paper-towel-lined plate.
9. **Optional pan sauce**: cook shallots or garlic in the pan until fragrant; add wine or liquor and the reserved juices; add optional flavourings, then swirl in butter, herbs and lemon juice as desired. Season to taste; add water and stir if needed to emulsify.
10. Serve immediately.

**Probe and endpoint**: the source states bath choices rather than a probe pull: **150°F / 66 °C for 1–4 h** (firm, very juicy, slightly tough), **165°F / 74 °C for 1–4 h** (tender and very juicy), and **165°F / 74 °C for 4–8 h** (fall-off-the-bone tender). The recipe setting displayed is 165°F / 73.9 °C for 1 hour. **no separate internal serving temperature stated** — in sous vide the meat equilibrates to the bath.

## Temperature consensus

| Tradition | Preferred doneness name | Final serving temp |
|-----------|------------------------|--------------------|
| US (Anova / J. Kenji López-Alt) | "tender and very juicy" at 74 °C / 165 °F bath; "fall-off-the-bone" at 74 °C for 4–8 h | Equilibrates to bath: 66–74 °C / 150–165 °F |

One fetched source establishes the method. Its chart spans 66–74 °C (150–165 °F) and is a
doneness menu rather than a single consensus: 66 °C produces the firmest, juiciest meat,
while 74 °C (the USDA safe temperature) pushed to 4–8 hours produces fall-off-the-bone
texture. In sous vide the bath temperature equals the final serving temperature after
equilibration, so unlike every other method here there is no pull-temperature/carryover
distinction to draw. The stated band overlaps the USDA safe line at its top end and the
cut's `dark_meat_optimal` band at 74 °C; nothing here supports a claim beyond this single
source.

## What makes this method special

Sous vide decouples pasteurisation from texture: the thigh spends its whole cook at the exact
target temperature, so 66 °C meat is safe by time while staying firm and juicy, and 74 °C
held long enough fully converts collagen without any risk of overshoot. The bone-in cut adds
only a little equilibration time and is explicitly favoured by the source, since the femur
keeps the thigh's shape during long holds. The necessary compensating step is the sear: the
bagged skin comes out pale and rubbery and must be crisped in a hot skillet, making this the
most two-stage method for this cut.

## Summary

Sous vide gives bone-in thighs precisely chosen doneness with zero overshoot, trading away all crust for a mandatory skillet finish. It is arguably the most controllable method for this forgiving cut, especially for the collagen-heavy 74 °C long-hold texture. The single US source's chart is the only temperature evidence so far, so no general target can be claimed.
